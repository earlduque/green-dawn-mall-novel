## 04 · don't
### *The Tower*

> *ATTENTION SHOPPERS: for your safety, please hold the handrail and attend your children. Do not use in the event of. Signs posted below this one were not posted by us.*

Burt went down first, because nobody else was going to.

That was the whole of his reasoning and he was comfortable with it. Somebody had stood at the top of this escalator on their own, written four lines on the back of a poster, hidden it in a rack, and gone back the way they came. Alex had the card in her coat pocket now. Whatever else was true, a person had been up here by themselves with a decision to make, and the thing that had happened to them was that there had been nobody in front of them.

So he got the pack up on both shoulders, and shifted the paper sack under one arm, and offered it round one more time first, which took about a quarter of a minute and got him nothing.

"On the way," Stacy said. "I eat on the way."

"You don't, though."

"I might."

He counted them off the fountain the way he counted a crew off a truck. Five. Five was what he'd started the day with, which he was aware was a low bar, and which he intended to keep clearing.

The handrail was cracked black rubber and it was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a car seat is warm — hand temperature, the temperature of something somebody had just let go of. Burt laid four fingers on it and left them there, because the sign at the top said to hold the handrail and Burt held handrails.

The treads came up out of the comb plate one after another with a sound he knew. Chain, sprocket, a small tick at the top of every cycle where a link went round something it had gone round a great many times. He listened to it for the first three steps and made a decision about it, which was that this machine was fine. Somebody had greased it. Somebody was still greasing it.

"It's working," he said, over his shoulder. "That's a maintained unit."

"Great," Alex said. "Wonderful. Love that."

"You say that like it's nothing. Somebody comes down here with a grease gun."

He did not say the rest of the thought, which was: *and who signs off on that, and where's the log.* He had a habit of wanting the log. It had not been useful yet.

---

Hank came on second with the camera up, walking backward down a moving stair, which Burt watched with the particular calm of a man who has decided he will simply catch this individual if it comes to that. Stacy came third, sideways, one hand on each rail, heels set crosswise on the treads. Alex fourth. Djan last, three steps back, not touching anything.

The throat of it went down a long way. The light down there was the same light as up here, which took the shine off the going-down. Normally you descend toward something dimmer. That was a small thing and Burt let it go past.

Under the ventilation the piano went round again, the little climb and the little fall, and it had come up in volume the way a radio does when you walk from the kitchen into the room the radio's in. He had it timed in his head at about thirty seconds now. It was going to be in there for a while.

The unease came in around the middle of the flight.

It did not arrive attached to anything. That was the part Burt would have written down if he'd been writing anything down. It was the feeling he got on a site when a load was over torque and nobody had said so yet, and there was nothing under it — no sound, no smell, no give in the tread. Just the certainty, down at the animal level, that this was a place to be watching your hands.

The wall panels on the left had a lean in them. He noticed it the way you notice a picture hung crooked, and he put his eye along the seam to check it, and by then the seam was straight.

"Everybody good," he said.

"Yeah," Hank said, still filming.

"Djan."

"Good."

There were columns down the shaft at even spacings, square, clad in beige tile, and one of them was thinner than the others. Not by much. By the width of a hand, maybe, the way a stud comes out wrong at the end of a run when somebody's cutting to the last board they've got. He counted the tiles across the face of it and got the same number he'd got on the one before it.

Behind him Stacy said, "Nope," conversationally, to nothing.

Something moved off at the edge of his vision, on the right, at about the height of a person walking, and when he turned his head there was tile.

He faced front again. There was a way to handle this and he knew what it was. You do not go and look. You keep the crew on the stair, you keep your hand on the rail, you get to the bottom, and if there's a thing there when you get there, you deal with the thing that's there. Half the injuries he'd ever seen had happened to a man who turned around on a ladder.

"Talk to me about something," he said.

"About what?"

"I don't care. Hank. Tell me about the camera."

Hank told him about the camera. It took most of the rest of the way down, and it involved the shoulder pad and the tape door and a year, and Burt did not follow one word in four, and it was exactly what he had asked for.

---

The floor at the bottom was sticky.

Not wet. Sticky — the tread of his boot came away from it with a small sound, and did it again on the next step, and the third step in it stopped. It ran three, maybe four paces out from the comb plate and then it was ordinary tile.

Burt stopped and looked back at it while the others came off, and put a hand out to slow Stacy at the right moment, and got her past it on the dry side.

"What," she said.

"Floor's tacky. Right there."

"Tacky how?"

"Somebody spilled something." He got his boot up and looked at the sole and there was nothing on it. "A long time ago, and nobody came out and did it."

That was the thing that got him, standing at the base of a working escalator in a building with the lights on. Somebody greased the chain. Nobody mopped the floor. Those were two different people and one of them had quit.

The sign on the wall said LEVEL TWO, in the same clean lettering as the one upstairs that said LEVEL ONE.

Alex said, "Two under one," to herself, filing it.

Then Hank said, "Guys," and they all went and looked at the corridor mouth.

There were two signs on the wall beside it.

The top one was a board, hand-painted, white ground and green letters with a brush edge you could see, and it said `LOWER LEVELS →`. Somebody had taken time over it. The arrow had been laid in with a straightedge and the paint had been given a second coat.

Under it, on the wall itself, in red marker, small, in a different hand:

`don't`

Nobody said anything for a moment. Burt stood in front of it with his thumbs under the pack straps and worked the only question he had.

Which one went up second.

Because if the board came first, then somebody had come along after, with a marker they happened to have on them, and written a correction. And a correction means a person learned something. But if the marker came first — if somebody had put *don't* on a bare wall, and then somebody else had come along and painted a board with an arrow on it and hung it on top — then that was a different situation entirely, and Burt did not have a name for it.

There was no way to tell. Paint doesn't date and marker doesn't either.

"Hank," Alex said. "Get both of those."

Hank got both of those.

"We're not taking advice off a wall," Alex said.

"No," Burt said. "But somebody wrote it."

---

The plaza was the same shape as the one upstairs and it was not the same room.

Six sides, corridors let into two of them, storefronts around the rest. No fountain. Where a fountain would have gone there was floor, polished, with a ring of darker tile set into it about twelve feet across, which was either a design decision or the outline of a thing that had been taken out.

It was still decorated. That was the word Burt reached for and it was the wrong one and he could not get to a better one. There was bunting up along the top of the storefronts, and there were tinsel garlands twisted round the handrails, and there were paper letters strung on a line spelling something he could read from the wrong side, and none of it was for anything. It was not Christmas. It was not a sale. Somebody had put all of that up with a staple gun and a ladder, for a reason, and the reason had gone.

He looked up while he was thinking about the ladder, because that was where his eyes went, and the ceiling was closer than the Atrium's had been. Up there you couldn't find it at all. Here it was a grid of white panels maybe eighteen feet off the deck, giving off even light with no lamp in it. He filed that where he filed things and said nothing about it, because a ceiling is not a hazard.

Two corridors. The one on the left had another escalator in it, going down, which Burt could see from here and did not care for. The one on the right went about forty feet and turned, and past the turn there was light and no information at all.

Three units with their lights on. A record shop with a sign that said Discworld and racks of sleeves in the window. A bookshop, PageTurn, deep, with the shelves running away from the glass. And on the far side, watches and clocks, hung and stood and laid out in rows behind the window of a place called Tangled Time.

Burt did the count without thinking about it. Five. Two ways on, three doors, and behind him a machine that only went one direction.

Djan was already twenty feet toward the right-hand corridor.

"Nope," Burt said. "Not by yourself."

Djan came back. He came back at a pace that made it clear he was doing Burt a favor, but he came back.

Stacy had her bag open.

---

She got the deck out and did not make a thing of it. Just squared the box in her hand, thumbed the flap, and knelt down on the polished floor in a way that cost her something in that skirt.

"Okay," she said. "I need everybody to shut up for a minute."

They shut up.

Burt watched it happen and thought it was interesting. He had said that sentence to grown men on a site and been ignored. Alex had her hand half up and put it down. Hank lowered the camera to his hip and left it running, which for Hank was silence.

Stacy looked more surprised than anybody.

She laid them out on the floor in a fan, one at a time, face down, and touched the first with two fingers before she turned it.

"Escalator down," she said, and turned it. She looked at it for a second longer than she'd looked at the others' worth of nothing. "That's the Tower."

"That's bad," Hank said.

"That's not good."

She moved to the second. "Corridor we can't see into. The Hierophant." A beat. "That one's calm, actually. That's fine."

The third. "Records. The Lovers."

The fourth she turned and stopped with her hand still on it. "PageTurn. Page of Cups."

"Meaning."

"Page of Cups. That's a lost friendship." She said it flatly, and did not perform it, which she almost always did. "We go there first."

The fifth she turned last and looked at longest, and it was the clock shop. "Hanged Man," she said, and put her hand over it, and gathered all five back into the deck without letting anybody else look at them.

Alex said, "Hold on. Lovers is freedom, right? That's a freedom card. Somebody in the record shop needs getting out of something."

"Maybe."

"That's an actionable card. A lost friendship is a mood."

"It's a lost friend, Alex."

"It's a card."

Stacy got up off the floor, straightened her skirt with two efficient passes, put the deck back in the bag under the makeup bag, and started walking to PageTurn.

Alex was still talking. Burt went after Stacy, because when somebody is walking, somebody should be behind them. That was not a position on the record shop. He'd have gone after Alex the same way.

Behind him he heard Alex stop mid-sentence and follow, and Hank come up, and Djan last, and that was five.

---

The bookshop had a chime, and the chime did not go off.

The door swung, and the air came out at them, and the sound in there was less than the sound outside by more than a door's worth. Burt felt the piano go from *around him* to *somewhere above*, and stopped in the doorway a second because the drop in it was physical, like the moment a diesel shuts off.

Long shelves, seven of them, running back further than the shopfront had any business allowing. Carpet with a nap on it. Paper — that dry smell, all of it, floor to ceiling.

Three people were standing at three different shelves.

They were not together. There was a woman in a raincoat halfway down the second aisle, and a man in a work shirt at the end shelf, and somebody young at the back, and each of them had a book open in both hands, and each of them was turning pages. Forward. Then back. Then forward. Then back.

Not reading. Burt had watched enough people work to know the difference between a hand that's doing a job and a hand that's just going. Their eyes weren't tracking. The pages went over at the same rate on all three, near enough, and the sound of it was three papery ticks slightly out of step, like a shop with three clocks in it.

The woman in the raincoat was crying. Her face was wet from the eyes down and had been for a while, judging by the collar, and she did not put a hand up to it, and she did not sniff, and she turned the page forward and then she turned it back.

"Ma'am," Burt said.

Nothing.

At the far end of the shop somebody was shelving. A figure in a hood, back to them, working along a low shelf with a cart, taking books off the cart and putting them up, unhurried. The hood came forward far enough that there was no face in it from this angle. Burt watched the hands go up and down twice before he registered what he was seeing about them, which was gloves.

"Hey," Hank said, to the hood, and started that way, because that was what Hank did.

There was a counter along the left wall with a pad and a pen on it and a stool pushed in.

The phone on the counter began to ring.
