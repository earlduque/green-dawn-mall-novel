## 06 · The Library Only Checks In

> *ACQUISITIONS THIS PERIOD: A Practical Guide to the Balancing of Stones · Field Producing for Television · American Convertibles 1979–1984 · Famous Deagents of the Past · The Journal of a Mall Explorer, by Jordan.*

The book on the counter was not square to the counter, and Djan had crossed the room to look at it while the dial tone was still going, and had not told anyone he was going.

Everything else in PageTurn lined up. The shelf-ends were flush. The spines had been pushed back to a line you could have run a string along. The wire stand of returns slips at the corner of the counter sat with its base parallel to the edge underneath it, and Djan had watched the librarian do that part — gloved hands, one slip at a time, the flat of the palm bringing the stack true.

The book was off by a few degrees. It had been set down by somebody who was not going to come back and straighten it, and set down from the customer side, and in the carpet in front of it there were two shallow pits where a person had stood with their weight on their heels for a while and then gone.

Djan looked at the carpet longer than he looked at the book. Then he took one photograph of the counter from the side, with the pits in the frame, and said nothing at all.

---

Green cloth, the title stamped on the front in gold, the gold gone thin at the tops of the capitals the way it goes on a book that has been shelved and pulled and shelved again.

*The Journal of a Mall Explorer.* Underneath, smaller: *by Jordan.*

A catalogue number inked on the foot of the spine. A pocket glued inside the back board with a card in it, ruled into columns for dates, every column empty. A copyright page with a printing history four lines long. And on the back board, at the top, a rectangle of pale paper where an author photograph goes, with the word *Jordan* printed in italic beneath it, and nothing in the rectangle at all.

"That's his notes," Hank said. He had come up on Djan's right with the Betacam already off his shoulder. "That's the notebook. It's a spiral. It's a spiral notebook."

"It isn't now."

Alex arrived last. Her right hand was still wrapped to the knuckles in Burt's gaffer tape and she was carrying it away from her body a little, the way you carry a hand that has been somewhere.

"Can we take it," she said, to the back of the store.

The librarian was shelving. He had an armful and he was working along a low row, and he did not stop, and the hood did not turn.

"It's been checked in."

"So check it out to us."

"There's nowhere for it to go."

Hank tried it anyway — the flat of his hand on the cover, a half-inch lift — and put it back down and wiped his palm on his jeans without appearing to notice he had. "Fine," he said. "Then we film all of it. Every page. Alex turns."

Alex turned the pages with the taped hand, one at a time, slow enough for the tape. Hank stood over her shoulder with the camera braced on the counter. Stacy read out loud from the other side. Burt stood where he could see the door and the three people at the shelves.

Djan did not read. He watched the pages come over and let them accumulate, the way you look at a contact sheet: not for the picture, for what the pictures have in common.

They had this in common. It was all typeset — narrow serifs, justified, pages numbered top-outside. A person had written this in a mall with a ballpoint pen, and the mall had set it in type, and had done a professional job of it.

---

The map was a plate, printed on heavier stock, with a rule around it and a caption in italic underneath that said *Fig. 1.*

Boxes stacked down the page and joined by arrows. At the top, ENTRY. Then a box with a circle in it and the circle hatched over with coins: *Plaza One.* Then *Plaza Two*, and out in the margin beside it, reproduced in Jordan's cramped print, the letter T's crossed with a bar floating above them: *sticky steps third from bottom, avoid.*

"Third from the bottom," Burt said.

"What?"

"On the way in." He was looking at the margin note like a man reading a permit. "Floor was tacky at the bottom of that escalator. Third step. I felt it go on my boot." He straightened up. "Somebody knew about that and wrote it down. That's what you do." Then, after a second: "Nobody put a sign on it, though. That's a trip hazard on a moving stair. You cone that off."

Nobody answered him. Stacy put a hand flat on his back for a moment and took it away again.

Then *Plaza Three*, which was a box drawn with a ruler, with a label line ruled underneath it and nothing written on the line.

Then the last box, circled twice, one circle inside the other, both gone over so hard that the typesetter had reproduced the ridges as a thicker stroke. A star beside it. LEVEL FOUR. In capitals across the box, *THE ROOM. THE SCREENS.*, and under that, smaller, running out of room and shrinking to fit: *it's here, I know it's here.*

Below the last box the plate was white to the rule.

"That's the whole map," Alex said.

"That's the whole map."

She turned the page. The next page was text.

---

It came a third of the way in, in the middle of a paragraph, with no heading, the way a person writes down a thing they have been carrying around all day.

*It films what you feel, not what you see. If I can get it out, nobody can ever lie again.*

"That's a hell of a camera," Hank said.

Djan stood with his hands at his sides and understood it completely.

He was good at his trade in the one way that mattered, which was that he could wait — stand in a doorway with the camera at his chest until the room forgot about him, and then get the frame where a person's face was doing what their face actually did. He had a print at home of a man at a bus stop who had just learned something, and he had never once been able to say what. That was the whole of the trade. You got the outside of it, beautifully, at a thousandth of a second, and the inside stayed inside.

A camera that got the other thing.

He found that his right hand had come up and settled on the body of his own camera, and he took it off again.

"Sure," he said. "It's a hell of a camera."

---

The final entry was three days old and it was the last page with anything on it.

*It found me first. My hand is plastic to the wrist. I can't feel it at all — that's the part that's worse. The camcorder is in my right hand. I'm not putting it down. I don't think I can anymore. The room is full of screens. All of them are showing the mall. Some of them are showing me. I think I'm on them. Don't develop the tape without me. Promise me. Whoever finds this — Come down.*

The ventilation went on doing what it did. Under it the Jingle finished the little fall and started again at the top.

Hank kept the camera on the page for a long time after he had it.

Then Burt got the paper sack out of the backpack and opened the top of it and held it out, and it went around all four of them and came back to him with nothing taken, and he folded the top down again and put it away.

Alex closed the book. Then she moved it with two fingers of the taped hand until it was square to the counter.

Djan watched her do that and did not mention the pits in the carpet.

---

The shelves ran the length of the store and there was nothing on the shelf-ends. No letters, no numbers, no subject cards in the brass frames. The frames were empty and they had been polished.

At his own eye level, at the third bay along, there was a green spine with *A Practical Guide to the Balancing of Stones* on it in white.

He had not been looking for it. He had been counting bays.

The cover had a photographic plate on it: four stones on a shore, stacked, the top one standing on a point the width of a thumbnail. Whoever had shot it had understood the job. You could see the shadow under the contact.

The bay held other things. *Field Producing for Television*, at Alex's height, which she found by walking into it and then stood in front of with her arms crossed. *American Convertibles 1979–1984.* A grey one called *Famous Deagents of the Past.*

"Nope," Stacy said, from the far end. She had read a spine and gone back a full step from it, on the heels, without looking down, which for Stacy was a feat. "Nope. Not today. I don't want it."

"What is it?"

"Doesn't matter."

Burt was not looking at any shelf. He was watching the three people who were already holding books, and after a while he said, "She's cold. That one. Look at her arms." He shifted the pack. "Has anybody got a coat that's not doing anything?"

---

The woman was at the second bay, and she was crying without any indication that she knew it, and she was turning pages. Forward. Back. Forward. Back.

Djan had been listening to that since they came in. It was not the sound of somebody reading. It had a beat in it, and the beat went round in about thirty seconds, and it was the same climb and the same fall that was coming out of the ceiling, and all three of them at all three shelves were on it together.

He had noticed that a while ago. He had not brought it up.

"Give me that wand," Burt said.

Djan took the remote out of his inside pocket and handed it over. Burt found the top button and cycled it — orange, white, a red that came out pink in here — and stopped on green, because green was where Djan had left it and Burt was not a man who fiddled.

He pointed it at the book in the woman's hands and pressed.

The book left her. Not thrown and not dropped — taken, out and down, the way a thing goes when somebody you cannot see takes it off you. It hit the carpet spine-up with the pages open under it and lay there.

Her hands stayed where they were, keeping the shape of it, thumbs up, the fingers a little apart.

Then she came apart. She made a noise Djan had heard twice before in his life, both times outdoors, and got both arms around Burt, who took it standing, with the pack still on, and patted her upper back three times with the flat of his hand. She was talking the whole time. The accent was heavy and the thanking came through it anyway, more times than the room could hold, and she was already moving toward the door while she said it.

Djan stepped into her way, one step, and put nothing in his voice.

"Have you been to Level Four?"

She stopped. Something went across her face.

"Oh God, there's a Level Four?"

She did not wait to hear whether there was. She went round him — then stopped again, four feet from the door, and put her hand into her coat pocket, and came out with a stack of Polaroids.

They were grey. All of them, the whole square of the frame, an even grey with no image coming up in it and none going out. Nothing written on any of the white borders. She held them out and Djan took them, because she was going to let go of them either way.

"Where are these from?"

She looked at the stack in his hands with an expression he could not put anywhere, and said she didn't know.

"When did you get them?"

She said she didn't know, and the second time she was frightened, and then she was through the door and out into the concourse, moving well.

Djan squared the stack against his palm and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, which was empty, because Burt had the remote.

---

Burt did the man at the first bay next. The book came out of his hands and hit the carpet, and he blinked twice, and looked at the five of them, and thanked them the way you thank somebody who holds a door. Then he tucked his shirt in at the back and walked out into the mall.

Burt did the third one. The book left his hands.

"I was reading that!"

He took another one off the shelf at chest height without looking at the spine, and opened it, and started to flip. Forward. Back. Forward. Back.

Burt lifted the remote again. That book went into the carpet too, and the man said *I was reading that* in exactly the same voice, and took a third off the shelf.

Burt stood there with the remote at the end of his arm.

"Burt," Alex said.

He came.

The librarian went past them with an armful, and stooped, and picked the books up off the carpet, and shelved them.

---

Three people had come into this store and picked up books. Two had walked out. One had picked up another one. And not one of the five of them knew what the books actually did, which meant every store and every counter and every object anybody handed them from here on was going to be decided by people guessing.

There was a way to stop guessing.

Djan waited until they were most of the way to the door. Then he went back four steps to the third bay and took *A Practical Guide to the Balancing of Stones* off the shelf and opened it in the middle.

It was good.

That was the part he had not been ready for. The chapter he had opened into was about the moment when the top stone finds the place — not the balance, the finding, the half-second where your fingers know before you do — and it described that better than Djan had ever described it to anybody, including himself, and it went on describing it, and there was another page, and the pages were about him.

He had a garden wall at home with four stacks standing on it and nobody had ever asked him a question about them.

He was aware of the store, and of Alex saying his name from the doorway in the voice she used when she already knew. He walked out with them, because he had every intention of walking out with them.

"Djan."

"Don't worry," he said. "I'll catch up."

The concourse carpet came up under his shoes. Burt got in front of him and there was a green light going across the page, and Djan put a shoulder into him and kept walking, and Burt came round the other side, and Djan shouldered him off again. He was not angry about it. He just did not want to be interrupted, in the way you do not want to be interrupted when somebody is finally telling you the thing correctly.

His thumb was riding the outside edge of the pages. Forward, back, forward, back, the pages going over on the climb and coming back on the fall, and up in the ceiling the piano did the little climb and the little fall, and Djan knew what he was doing and could hear that he was doing it in time.

He had stood in that store and watched a woman do exactly this.

He noted the match. He filed it where he filed things.

The taped hand came across from his left with no wind-up at all, and the book went out of his hands and into the carpet, spine-up, pages open underneath it, and lay there exactly the way hers had.

The ventilation. The Jingle, starting again at the top. Four people standing in a semicircle looking at him and Alex shaking out her fingers inside the tape.

"Thank you," Djan said. "Back to reality."

Then his hand went to the inside pocket of his jacket, and pressed once, flat, against the stack of Polaroids through the cloth, and he did not mention them to anybody.
