## 05 · Page of Cups

> *PAGETURN · PLEASE NOTE. This location accepts returns. This location does not process loans. Items may be checked in at any hour. There is no fine, and there is no limit, and there is nothing to take home.*

Alex let it ring.

She had come three steps in and stopped there, and the phone on the counter had gone through four rings in that time, and nobody in the room had looked at it. That was the fact worth having. A ringing phone is a question one person is asking another. She wanted to know which of them was being asked before she picked it up and made herself the answer.

"You going to get that," Stacy said, quietly, because the room made you quiet.

"In a minute."

"It's been ringing since we walked in."

"I know how long it's been ringing."

Long shelves ran back from the door in rows at head height, full, with no gaps and nothing left over at the ends. The carpet took the sound out of five sets of shoes at once. It smelled of paper — dry, the smell of a place where books have sat in the same order for a long time — and under that, faint, the plastic.

The quiet had weight. Alex had been in libraries. This was not that. In a library the quiet is a thing people are keeping up. Here it had settled, the way silt settles.

Three people stood at three shelves with three books open.

The nearest was a woman in a raincoat, fifties, hair pinned and coming down out of the pins. She had a hardback flat on both palms and she was turning pages. Forward, three or four. Back, the same three or four. Forward. Back. Her eyes went across the page each time, top to bottom, as though it were new.

She was crying. Not sobbing — running. Both cheeks were wet to the jaw and the collar of the raincoat had gone dark at the front, and her breathing never caught, and her hands never stopped, and at no point did she bring a wrist up to her face.

Past her, a man in a windbreaker with a paperback. Past him, at the far end, somebody young in a shirt and tie holding a book with a red cover. Both of them doing it. Forward, back, forward, back.

They were not in unison. They were on the same beat, and the beat was the phone. The pages went forward on the ring and came back in the space between.

At the back of the store a figure in a hood was shelving books off a cart. The garment went to the floor and had sleeves and Alex would not have wanted to name it further than that. Tall. Unhurried. He took a book off the cart, found its place, put it in, and squared it up. He did not turn around.

Djan stood looking at the cart. Then he went on looking at it, and said nothing about it.

Alex took all of that in and set it aside, because there was one job in this room. Jordan had stood on this carpet. Somebody in here had to look up.

---

Burt set the bags down, which took a while, and went to the woman in the raincoat.

He did it the way he did everything, which was slowly and from the front, where she could see him coming if she had been looking. He crouched a little to get under her eyeline. He did not touch her at first.

"Ma'am."

Forward. Back.

"Ma'am, is everything all right? Seems like you could use a friend right now."

He put a hand on her shoulder, flat, the way you steady a ladder. She did not shrug it off. She did not register it. The raincoat moved under his palm with her breathing and that was all that happened.

"What are you reading, hon?"

Forward. Back.

Stacy came up on the other side and went down on her heels, which in those shoes was a decision, and put her face in the woman's field of view and held it there. Faces were Stacy's whole trade. She could get a shopkeeper to say a thing on camera he had refused to say off it. She said, "Hi. Hi. I'm Stacy," in the voice she used to open doors with, and the woman's eyes came down the page, top to bottom, and went back up to the top.

"She's looking right through me," Stacy said. "I don't mean she's ignoring me. There's nothing behind it."

Hank had the camera on his shoulder and did not raise it, and then raised it.

Alex ran the list she would have run anywhere else. Get a name. Get a next of kin. Get somebody to sit with her, get her a glass of water, get her outside into air, call it in and hand her off to people whose job it was. Every item on the list needed the woman to look up.

Five of them stood around a person who was warm and breathing and eighteen inches away, and the width of that eighteen inches was the whole thing.

Burt looked at the book instead, since the woman was not available to be looked at. "Pages are worn," he said. "Right along the bottom corner. That's a thumb doing that."

"Don't," Alex said.

"I'm not touching it. I'm telling you what I see."

"Take the book off her," Hank said.

"No," Alex said.

"Why not?"

She did not have a reason yet. She had the shape of one, and she had learned in a lift that the shape of a sentence with the words taken out is still worth respecting. "Because we don't know what it is."

"It's a book."

"Then you take it."

Hank did not take it.

---

Burt straightened up out of his crouch. He looked at the woman's hands. Then he looked down the length of the store at the figure with the cart, and he looked for long enough that Alex turned to see what he was looking at.

"Hold on," he said.

He walked four rows down, stopped at a respectful distance, and came back.

"He's got gloves on."

"What?"

"The fella at the back. He's got gloves on. Cotton ones, the sort for handling photographs." Burt tipped his chin at the raincoat. "She hasn't. He has. Nobody else in here has." He said the next part in the flat baritone he used for weight limits. "I don't think we should touch the books."

Nobody said anything.

Hank took the camera off his shoulder and held it by the handle.

Alex looked at the back of the store, and the gloves came apart from the man and became the only piece of information in the room. One person in here was working. Four were not, and three of the four had their hands on paper, and the one who was working had put something between his skin and everything he touched.

She had been keeping a list since the lift. Down is an escalator and up is a staircase. The numbers go up as you go down. There were two things on it and she had put both of them there herself, and the third one had just been handed to her by a man who carried other people's bags.

"Nobody touches a book," she said. "Nobody touches anything of theirs."

"That's what I said."

"I know what you said, Burt. I'm saying it again so it's said twice."

"That's actually a great idea," Stacy said, standing up out of her crouch, "and I'm not doing any of it. I just had these done."

---

The phone rang.

"Somebody's got to pick that up," Alex said.

"Not with your hand," Burt said.

"It's a phone."

"It's their phone."

He was already going through the backpack, which was less a bag than a filing system, and what he came out with was the roll of gaffer tape. Silver, three inches, real, carried in from outside. He got the end up with a thumbnail and pulled, and the rip of it coming off the roll was the loudest thing that had happened since the escalator.

The raincoat did not move. Forward. Back.

"Right hand."

She gave him the right hand.

He worked it like he was prepping a load, which is to say he did it in an order and did not talk while he did it. Across the palm first, twice, thumbing the edge down flat. Then round the wrist, two turns, snug and not tight. Then a strip up the back of the hand and over the knuckles, and then the thumb on its own, which took him three goes and a small tear with his teeth. It smelled of rubber and something hot, the smell you get off a new tire in a shop. He held her fingers apart with his own to get the tape between them and his hands were enormous and careful.

"That's going to pull when it comes off," he said. "Sorry in advance."

"It's fine."

"It's going to pull."

He reached for her left hand.

"A phone takes one hand, Burt."

He considered that with real seriousness. "One at a time anyway," he said, and put the roll away.

Alex made a fist and it went most of the way. The tape creaked, the way a jacket creaks. She flexed the fingers, and opened them, and pressed the taped palm against the edge of a shelf, and found that she could feel the pressure of it and nothing about the surface at all.

She did not say anything about that. She turned her hand over once, front and back, like somebody checking a glove for fit, and went to the counter.

---

Hank got there first, because that is what Hank does. He went straight past the counter and down the length of the store to the cart, and said, "Excuse me."

The hood came round. The hood was deep and Alex, four rows back, could see that there was a hood and could not see anything in it, and she did not go closer to check.

"Blonde kid," Hank said. "Glasses. Camera like this, only older."

"I haven't met him." The voice came out level and unbothered, a customer-service voice, a voice with a shift to finish. "He checked in a book."

"Checked in."

"A book." One gloved hand came out and indicated the counter, and went back to the cart.

"Not much of a library," Stacy said, "if you can't check anything out."

Something moved inside the garment that was probably a shrug. He slid a volume into a gap that had been waiting for it.

"It's worked out fine for me."

---

The phone was cream plastic with a coiled cord and a bank of small legends printed down the side of the keypad, the kind of phone that sits in the lobby of a building and knows which floor is calling. It had rung eleven more times while Burt did her hand. Alex had stopped counting and then started again.

She picked it up with the taped hand, which was clumsier than she had allowed for, and got it to her ear on the second try.

"It's down here!"

It was Jordan. It was Jordan's voice at the volume Jordan used in the office, the middle of a sentence, dry and close, a person leaning into a doorway to tell you a thing before they lost it.

"Past where the escalators stop, there's a room and the walls are—"

The Jingle came out of the earpiece.

It came at a level the earpiece should not have been able to make. The little climb and the little fall, cheerful, not very good, and every one of them heard it — Stacy's head went round, Burt's chin came up, Hank swore. Alex took the phone off her ear and held it out at arm's length and put it back.

"—I can't put it down but I can see it filming me right now and it shows—"

The Jingle.

"—don't develop the tape without me, okay? Don't develop it without—"

The dial tone.

She stood with the receiver against her ear for a while after that, listening to the tone, because there was nothing else to do with the next few seconds.

Behind her the pages turned. The phone was not ringing any more and they had not changed their time.

"That was Jordan," Stacy said. "Tell me that was Jordan."

"That was Jordan."

"Say it again. The last bit."

"Don't develop the tape without me." Alex heard her own voice do it and did not like the flatness in it. "And then it cut."

Burt got the paper sack out of the backpack and opened it and offered it round, and nobody took anything, and he folded the top down and put it back.

Alex set the receiver in the cradle and squared it to the edge of the counter and started talking before anybody else could.

"Hank, the counter. Everything on it, both sides of everything, don't touch it. Djan, both corridors, count the doors, come straight back. Stacy, you're on him — the call, when, how many, what he heard. Burt." She stopped. "Burt, do your own hands."

"I don't need—"

"Do your own hands."

There was a sentence available to her. She could feel the room make a space for it, the way rooms do, and she knew the shape of it and roughly how long it would take to say, and she had known both of those things since a Tuesday two weeks ago. She used the space to lean over the keypad instead.

The legends ran down the side in a column, five of them, and one was lit.

"Level Four," she said.

"That's two below us," Burt said.

"One below the sign that said don't," Stacy said.

Nobody built anything on top of that.

---

"The further down you go," the librarian said, from the shelves, without being asked, "the more of that you'll get."

"More of what."

"What you're worried about." He shelved a book. "There's a Screening Room on Level Four. I'm aware of it. I've never been down."

Alex came four rows closer and stopped at the end of the aisle. "You've talked to him."

"I spoke to your friend."

"When?"

"Three days ago. On that phone."

"Did he sound like that?" She tipped her head back toward the counter. "Like just now."

The cart was pushed six inches along the carpet. "I couldn't say."

"You couldn't say, or you don't remember."

"I couldn't say," he said, and there was nothing under it, no evasion she could get her fingers into, no seam. It was the answer of a man reporting the limit of what he had.

Stacy said, "Who checked the book in?"

"Not me."

"You said Jordan checked it in."

"I said it was checked in." He put a book away. "It was on the counter when I turned around."

Alex stood still.

"You didn't see anybody come in."

"I wasn't watching. It doesn't matter who brings a book in." He reached for the next one. "It's in."

In her coat pocket, against her hip, there was a postcard with a distorted painting on the front of it, and on the back of it nine floating bars over nine letter T's in a hand that leaned away from itself. She had put it there in a poster shop one Level up. She did not take it out.

Hank was at the counter with the camera down. Djan had come back from the corridors and had not reported. The pages went forward and came back, forward and came back, three people at three shelves, and none of them was Jordan, and there was nothing in this room that any of the five of them could do about any of it.

The journal was where he had gestured, on the counter beside the phone. A hardback the size of a hand, cloth boards, a label on the spine, and a name printed on the label in type.

Alex put both hands behind her back, the taped one and the other one, and looked at it.

Somebody had walked in here while the only person in the room was facing the shelves, and set that down, and not stayed long enough to straighten it, and gone.
