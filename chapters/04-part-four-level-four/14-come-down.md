## 14 · Come Down

> *LEVEL FIVE. Employees only beyond this point. If you are able to read this sign, you are an employee. Congratulations on your promotion.*

Alex had paid for all five of them out of her own coat pocket, and she had been standing outside the shop a while now trying to work out what she had bought.

Four postcards and a magnet. That was the receipt version. A roll of quarters broken open on a counter, and a child who counted it twice and then gave some of it back as a discount nobody had asked for.

And these, out of the register drawer, handed round like raffle tickets.

It was a green oval the size of a bottle cap, die-cut with a scalloped edge, GREEN DAWN REWARDS across the top in a typeface that had been cheerful in some other decade. Under the lettering, a line for a name, and nothing on the line. The back was waxed paper with a slit up the middle for a thumbnail. It smelled the way the inside of a new appliance smells, which by now was not information.

She had been running the same sum since a poster rack four Levels up. What does it cost, who pays, is the thing on the far side worth the price. She had never once got it to come out, because the term she needed always turned out to be a person.

Nobody had peeled theirs. Five adults stood in a gallery holding five green ovals while the walls painted themselves.

"So we're doing this," Stacy said.

"We're considering it."

"Alex." Stacy had hers up between two fingers like something she was about to bid with. "It's a sticker."

---

She got a thumbnail into the slit and peeled it and put it on her shirt, above the pocket, and pressed it flat the way anybody does.

It was not there.

"You dropped it," Hank said.

"I did not drop it, I *pressed* it—" and she had the front of her shirt pulled out and was looking down into it, which under other circumstances would have cleared the room.

Then she stopped. "Oh," she said. "Oh, no."

Burt got there first, because Burt goes toward things. Stacy was holding her collar open with both hands, and Alex, from three feet back, could see it fine.

It was on the skin, two inches under the collarbone, where the sticker would have been if there had been no shirt in the way. The same oval, the same scalloped edge, the letters the right way round and not reversed, and a line for a name with nothing written on it. Not raised. Not red. It looked printed rather than drawn. It had gone through the cotton and come out the other side, and there was no mark on the shirt anywhere at all.

"It didn't touch the blouse," Stacy said.

"That's the part you're leading with."

"It's the part I can do anything about." She let the collar go and smoothed it and did not look down again. "Somebody else say something."

---

Alex had been the one who said nobody touches anything of theirs. She had said it twice, in a bookshop, on purpose.

She looked at her arm — the left one, inside, above the wrist, where the skin is thin enough to show what runs under it. Then at her right hand, which was silver, and had been silver long enough that she had stopped filing it as a separate object from the hand.

Then at the oval in her fingers.

"There's another way to think about this," she said, and could not produce one.

"You don't have to," Burt said.

"I know."

"I mean it. Four of us is plenty to look at a wall."

"Four is plenty for four people." Her thumbnail went into the slit and it came off the waxed paper with a small dry tick. "It isn't five."

That was the argument. She made it to a man who carried other people's bags, and he took it, because he would, and the whole of it ran to under a minute. She would go back over that minute afterward and never find the place where she should have made it longer.

She put it on the inside of her left forearm and pressed it, and there was nothing to feel. A warmth the size of a coin, for about as long as it takes to notice a warmth. Then her fingers were empty and her arm had a green oval on it with a line for a name.

She pulled the sleeve down over it.

Djan did his on the back of his wrist, watching it go, which was the closest thing to a ceremony anybody managed. Burt put his on the point of his shoulder through his shirt, like a man slapping a permit onto a window.

Hank did not put his on.

Nobody made him say anything about it. He stood with it closed in his fist and the camera on his shoulder and looked at a spot in the middle distance, and when Stacy said his name he said, "Yeah," and his hand stayed shut, and that was all of it. Alex watched him not do it and had nothing available that would be worth what it would cost him to hear. He had come down four Levels going first at everything. This was the one, and it was a sticker.

"Okay," she said, and let him alone.

---

The kiosk was set into the wall between two units. The attendant came up off his stool as though he had been hoping.

"There we are," he said. "Oh, that's very good indeed."

"You can see it."

"I can *always* see it. That was never the difficulty."

He came out from behind the desk and put a hand toward the blank wall beside him, and there was no blank wall beside him.

There was an escalator, going down.

She looked at it properly, because she wanted the details before her eyes agreed to stop supplying them. Black rubber handrail, cracked across in one place. Treads gone shiny down the middle where feet land and still grooved at the edges where they don't. A wad of gum trodden flat on the third step and gone black at the rim. A laminated card screwed to the balustrade, asking her to hold the handrail and attend her children.

It had been there a long time. It had been there the entire time. Nobody had built it in the last four minutes, and the only thing that had changed in this plaza was four people.

"There's a wall," Hank said. He said it flatly, the way you read off a meter, then moved two feet to his left and looked again. "There's a wall."

---

He crouched by the kiosk, out of everybody's line, got his sock down over the top of his boot, put the oval on the back of his calf, pressed it, and put the sock back up.

He did not say anything while he did it, and nobody said anything either. Burt found something to look at near the ceiling, Stacy checked a strap that did not need checking, Alex looked at the escalator, and the four of them gave him the twelve seconds, which was the most any of them could do for him and was not nothing.

Then he stood up and his face changed.

"It's *worn*," he said.

"I know."

---

The attendant stepped aside for them, one pace back and one to the side, and he was still smiling, and Alex — who had spent a working life reading the difference between people who are pleased and people who are performing being pleased — looked a second longer than she needed to and got something she had not expected.

"Do you get many," she said.

"Not many," he said, and the smile stayed exactly where it was, and behind it he had gone somewhere else entirely.

Burt shook his hand. Alex watched that happen and did not stop it, and then could not decide what she thought about not stopping it.

Stacy went on first. Then Burt, then Djan, then Hank, and Alex last, because last is where you can see all four of them.

Six steps down, Djan turned and looked back up.

"Nice to know you," the attendant said, from further away than he should have been, in the voice of a man being shut behind a door.

Nobody answered. By the time Alex turned her head there was the top of the escalator up there, and the light, and nobody in it.

---

The steps were ordinary. That was the last ordinary thing.

Four treads down there was somebody standing to her left.

Not on the escalator. Out past the corner of her eye, at the place where an eye stops being able to say what it has — a person's height, a person's stillness, the general fact of a coat. Alex turned her head to look at it and it was on the other side.

She turned back. It went with her, or it had already been there, or there were two of them, and from inside her own skull the difference was not available. She put her eyes on the back of Burt's hard hat, three steps down, and held on to it, and the thing stood beside her the way a person stands beside you at a counter — patiently, not touching anything, waiting until you were free.

She did not look for a face. There was nothing in it for her.

"Is one of you talking," Stacy said.

"No."

"Somebody's talking." She had a hand up near her ear, not covering it. "It's far away and it's telling me things. Like a list. It's a *list*, it keeps going, it's not—" A step. "It's very calm."

Djan had both hands on the handrail and was standing with his knees soft, riding the steps the way you ride a boat.

"The floor won't stay," he said.

"Say more than that."

"It's like stepping off an elevator." His weight went and came back. "Twelve of them. One after another."

That was more than Djan usually gave anybody, and she was in the middle of being worried about that when the thing at the edge of her vision went away and an engine backfired six feet behind her.

She got the whole way round with the taped hand up before she understood there was nothing behind her but forty feet of moving stairs.

"Yeah," Hank said. "That one's mine."

"How long."

"Since the top."

---

It did not stay put.

The figure left and the backfiring took its place, and for four or five steps she was almost glad, because a backfiring engine is a thing that happens in a car park in the ordinary world and is therefore nearly good news. Then the floor let go. Not the steps — the steps were fine, she could feel the steps. Whatever the steps stood on had gone soft and was moving, the way ground moves when you have come off a boat and are pretending you haven't, and she took Djan's shoulder without asking and he let her have it.

Burt had a hand up on the brim of his hard hat, holding it down on his head, which she had not seen him do since he put it on.

"Who parked a *pool* there."

"There's no pool, Burt."

"There's a pool." He pointed off the side of the escalator at nothing. "Half dug. And they've got the water in already."

"In the hole?"

"Above it." He said it the way he said weight limits. "Hanging there. About a foot up. Nobody's signed off on that." A step. "And I've got two dozen men down there in waistcoats."

"In what?"

"Half of them are done up as waiters, and the rest are in—" he made a shape over his own head with one hand, a point, a brim — "wizards. Robes and the hats. You can't run a site like that."

It came round to her again, and she had time to see it coming, which was new and was worse: the pool went and the waistcoats went and there was a person standing just outside the reach of her left eye, and she looked at Burt's hat and counted the seams in it.

She tried to count the steps, because counting is what she does with a corridor and four other people, and there was nothing here that stayed countable long enough to become a number.

"It stopped," Stacy said.

"The voice?"

"It stopped and now it's the floor." Two steps. "I want the voice back."

"No you don't."

"I do, though." Both hands on the rail, bag hanging off one forearm. "It knew what it was doing. That's the — it was *organized*, Alex. It sounded like somebody who had a plan for me."

Nobody said anything to that. Alex, three steps above her, understood the appeal completely and was not going to be the one to say so.

Burt got the paper sack out and offered it up the steps and then down the steps, and nobody took anything, and he put it away.

The engine backfired. Hank jumped, and swore at himself for jumping.

It went round again after that, and again, and there stopped being any shape to the order of it. The figure. The floor. The engine. The voice, once, for her, for about two seconds — far off, level, mid-sentence, a tone and not words, and she got the tone and did not get the words and was grateful in a way that made her feel small. Then the figure again. Then the floor. It never got worse than it had been at the start. It never got better either. It kept being available, low and constant, the way a smell is, and the flash of relief every time it changed hands was the part she would think about afterward, because relief is a thing you can be trained with.

Then the steps ran out of themselves and flattened, that last half-inch that tells your knees, and the comb plate went by under her shoe, and it was over the way a phone call ends.

---

Every surface was a screen.

She was standing on one. Under her own boot was a corridor with the light on in it and nobody walking down it. The walls of the corridor mouth were screens set edge to edge with no bezel she could find, and the ceiling was a screen too, ten feet up, lower than the Gallery's had been.

Not one of them showed the room she was in. That was the first thing she looked for.

The rest showed the mall. A fountain going over its lip in a sheet. A sheet of ice with two teams on it and nobody in the seats. A corridor. Another corridor. Eleven screens in a row all showing the same escalator from eleven slightly different heights, with nobody on any of them.

Under the ventilation the piano did the little climb and the little fall, louder than it had ever been, close enough now that she could hear the room the recording had been made in. The plastic came up off everything, warm, the whole building's worth.

She catalogued the storefronts from where she stood and did not go a step nearer any of them. A shoe shop with the pairs mismatched in the window. A unit with brown paper taped over the glass, gone the colour of weak tea. A door with a bell above it, the bell wired open so it couldn't ring. Nobody came out of any of them.

"Alex," Djan said.

He was not looking at the shops.

---

At the far end were double doors. Grey paint over metal, a push bar across each, the pair of them shut and set flush into a wall of screens, so that the doors were the only thing on this Level not showing her something.

There was a sign on them. There were three.

Above the doors, a strip of card taped up at both ends, and on the card in black marker, in a wide steady hand that had left itself plenty of room: SCREENING ROOM.

Beneath it, on the metal itself, at head height, in white — a different hand, upright, patient, the letters even: EMPLOYEES ONLY.

And under that, low down, near the push bar, at the height of a person crouching.

Print, not cursive, and small. Capitals, cramped, the whole of it leaning back off the vertical, every downstroke pulled to the left as if the hand had been going away from the words while it made them. Blue ballpoint, pressed hard enough to have gone into the paint and left a furrow a fingertip could find. Neither word in it had a letter that took a bar across the top. The last one was smaller than the rest and crowded in, because the writer had run out of door and had put it there anyway:

COME DOWN

Alex looked at it for a while. She did not take anything out of her pocket.

What she had was a list of rules, four of them, kept in order with room after the last one. A brochure with the Level Five page left blank. A painted door four Levels up that opened onto a car park and held thirty seconds. A green oval under her sleeve with a line on it for a name, put there after an argument she lost on purpose in under a minute. And a thing somebody had asked of her down a phone line in a bookshop, about a tape, and not developing it, which nobody had yet said yes to out loud.

She was going to be the one who said they were going in. She knew it the way she knew the shape of a sentence before the words were in it, and she knew roughly how long it would take to say, and the other four were already turning to her, one two three four, waiting.

She did not say it yet.

The doors stayed shut at the end of the floor, dull as a fire exit, with three hands on them, and the lowest hand pressed hardest.
