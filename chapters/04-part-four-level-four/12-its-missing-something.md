## 12 · It's Missing Something

> *CANVAS & CRAFT — TRUE GREEN. Interior and exterior. One coat covers. Contains no pigment found in nature. Keep away from children. Dries to the touch in thirty seconds.*

"A knocker," Stacy said.

She heard it go out across the shop and land in front of four strangers in aprons, and there was nothing behind it at all. It was the voice she used for filling air when a guest was late. "You'd want a knocker on that. Brass. To match."

The lead painter looked at her. Grey to the elbow on one arm, a brush held down along his thigh like a cigarette he'd forgotten, and his face did what a face does when it is being polite about a wrong answer.

"It's no' a knocker," he said.

"No."

"It's a fine door, though." He turned back to it, and the four of them lined up again to look at what they had made, and she had lost them.

---

The door was six feet of wall and it was better than anything else in the building.

Five panels, oak, the grain running the way grain runs, one knot low on the left where a knot would be. A brass handle, the lever kind, on a plate with two screws in it whose slots were not lined up with each other, because that is how screws are. Under the handle, a shadow the length of a thumb, laid in by somebody who had done it many times.

"So what's it missing," Alex said.

"Aye," the painter said, which was not an answer, and took a brush off the pegboard.

---

Stacy went to work, because this was her thing.

Not the talking — anybody can talk. The skill she'd have put on a form was reading the quarter-inch. Faces leak. You put wrong guesses in front of a person until one lands and their face tells you so.

She got nothing off any of them, and she was watching the whole time.

"The paint's still wet," Hank said, up close with one eye shut. "That's it. It needs to dry."

Nobody had said Hank could help. The short painter, the woman with her hair up in a clip, put a coat on the bottom rail anyway, and the oak went deeper and more like oak.

"It's dry," she said.

"It's shining."

"It's dry."

Hank put a knuckle on it. He looked at his knuckle. He did not say anything about his knuckle.

"There's no hinges," Alex said. "It's a door with no hinges."

One of them went at the left edge with a brush the width of a pencil and put on three, and they were beautiful — the barrels catching the flat light in a line, the bottom plate a hair crooked the way the bottom one always is. Ninety seconds. Stacy watched the lead painter's face the whole time, and it was the face of a man watching hinges go on.

"That's not it either," Alex said.

"No."

Burt said a door wants a jamb and a stop and a sill, or it's just a lid. They painted the jamb. Now it sat in a frame with a lip of shadow down one side, and it was so far past being a picture of a door that Stacy caught herself planning the shot.

"Keyhole," Djan said.

They had all forgotten Djan was there, which happened.

"It has a handle and no keyhole. Everything else on it is right."

The tall one did the keyhole, and he was a long time about it, and when he stepped back there was a hole in the plate with a dark down inside it, and the dark had a bottom. She could not have told you how a person paints the bottom of a hole.

She put a finger on it. Wall.

"A doorbell," Hank said. "A mail slot. A number. One of those little signs with who lives there."

"Nobody lives there, Hank."

---

Her hand went into the bag on its own while she was still talking, and she got the corner of the box under two fingers, the split seam, the cardboard gone soft. Five cards. Everybody quiet for a minute.

She took her hand out and folded her arms.

Then she went and stood beside the lead painter, close enough to be a person and not close enough to be a problem, and told him the hinges were the best work she'd seen since she came in. He said *aye*, and did not stop moving, and she understood — with the part of her that always understood these two minutes before anybody else — that she wasn't being refused. There was nothing to refuse. He was busy, and she was weather.

She kept going a while after she knew. That was the part she'd think about later.

---

Burt had gone six feet back to look at the wall with his head tipped, the way a man looks at a ceiling he's going to need a ladder for.

"Fellas. That's a picture of a door." He put his hand flat on the panels. "But a door ain't the wood. A door's the hole. Wood's just what you put over the hole so the weather doesn't come in." He knocked twice, and it sounded like wall. "You've painted the part that keeps things out."

Nobody said anything.

"Stace. Give me your bag."

She gave him her bag.

---

He got it out with two fingers round the grip, and the whole crew stepped back without anybody suggesting it, because they had all seen it go once, in the cold. ICE DRILLER 3000, along the side, in letters designed by somebody at a company.

"That battery won't last," Alex said.

"This is the job."

He set the bit at about the height of a person's eye, dead centre, where a door has a peephole and this one did not. Stacy got both hands over her ears in time and it did not help.

It came up through the floor into her heels and through her hands into her skull and filled the shop end to end — the pigment bins ringing, the brushes walking sideways on the board, a jar going over somewhere at the back. Since the lift, under the ventilation, the piano had been doing its climb and its fall and its small stop; and for as long as Burt kept his thumb down the piano was gone, and Stacy stood in a hurricane of one man's noise and had the first quiet she'd had since she came into this building.

Nobody stopped him. Four people had spent a day on that wall and not one put a hand out.

Dust came off in a grey rope. The bit went in eight inches and then went in easy, and Burt let off the button, and the shop rang, and the piano started again from the beginning.

---

There was a hole in the door at the height of a person's eye.

Djan got to it first — simply there, one hand on the frame, before anybody had moved — and put his eye to it and held still for four or five seconds. Then he stepped away and let Hank in, and did not say one word about what he had seen.

The painters came at it like a pit crew, without talking, the short one working the frayed lip into a bevel and the tall one at the panel with a brush of six hairs, and while they worked the door stopped being an oak door.

It didn't fade. Nothing on that wall ever faded. It went from a picture of a door closed to a picture of the same door standing open, from the inside, and past the frame there was brightness — white lines on grey running away in ranks, grass beyond that going brown where the tarmac took over. Four cars parked in all that space, spread the way cars park when nobody has to think about it. One of them sat low on its back axle.

It came at her bent at the corners, small and wide at once, the way a peephole hands you the entire porch.

"That's outside," Hank said.

"Aye," said the lead painter, and put his hand on the brass and pushed the lever down.

His hand slid off it. There was no lever — a shape, a shine, a small painted shadow underneath, and eight inches of wall with a hole in it. One of the four put a hand up over his mouth.

That was the worst part of the day, and she could not have explained why. It was as real as a thing could get. And it did not open.

---

She was humming before she decided to.

It had been in her since the plaza — since she'd stood outside this shop with a fan of useless cards in her hands and heard music coming out of the doorway and known, with no argument for it, that this was the one. Not the piano. Something with more notes in it than the piano had.

"Okay," she said. "This is going to be embarrassing."

"Go on," said Alex.

She hummed it. She got four bars, put the fifth in the wrong place, said *sorry, hang on* — which she was fairly sure she had never said in front of a lens in six years — and started again from the top, out loud, in front of four strangers and every person she worked with.

The paint went dark.

It went dark the way water goes into cloth: the greys deepening, the white lines coming up brighter against them, the grass finding a colour it had not had a second before. A smell arrived, wet paint, sharp, at the front of her nose. The brass thickened. It stopped being a shine on a flat wall and became a thing with a top and a bottom, throwing a shadow of its own across the plate — and the painted shadow was still under it, so now there were two.

Djan reached out and stopped an inch short.

"Handle's real," Burt said.

"Yeah," Alex said, and got out her notebook, and Burt hitched the backpack, and the short painter clapped her two hands together once and then went and did something to the bevel.

Nobody made a fuss. Stacy waited a second for one, the way you wait at the end of a piece to camera for the count in your ear, and it didn't come, and she liked it better than the alternative. She had not laid a card.

---

The lead painter opened it, because it was his.

The lever went down with a sound, and the door came in toward the shop on three hinges that had been paint an hour ago.

The air came first. Warmer than the shop, and moving, with cut grass in it and hot tarmac and the ghost of somebody's exhaust, and it was not plastic. Stacy's mouth came open and she left it open. Out there a bird did four notes and quit.

Then the light.

It came through at an angle, one angle, and landed on the floor in a long bar with an end on it, and went up the side of the pigment tray and stopped. Past that, everything was as it had been all day. Every person in the room had a lit side and a dark side. Burt threw a shadow across two bins, and it was the only shadow in the building that had ever gone one way.

Six years of standing in car parks, checking her face in a compact, turning a few degrees to find the angle that worked. Here was the angle. Somebody had left it out here the whole time.

Hank's camera was on his hip. He did not put it up.

The floor of the shop and the tarmac were at the same height. There was no step between them.

"Four," Burt said. He said it again, quietly, the way a man counts a load. "Four steps. That's four steps and we're in the car park."

"Three, from where you are," Djan said.

"Three." Burt did not move. "Three steps."

Stacy, who had never once in her adult life been in a room without something to say in it, stood in real light and had nothing.

"He can't," Alex said.

She said it like a woman reading off a clipboard, notebook in the taped hand. "He's further down than this and he hasn't got a door. We go out there, we're five people in a car park with a story about a mall." A beat. "And he's still in it."

Djan raised his camera, took one photograph of the car park, and let it drop on the strap.

"It'll still be here," Hank said.

"Sure," Alex said.

"It will. She can do it again. We know how it works now."

"Sure."

The bird did not do it again. Stacy stood in the warm bar of light and worked out that she could be in the driver's seat inside a minute — and that she was thinking about the drive and not about the getting out, which meant she had already decided, which meant they all had, and there was going to be no announcement.

Burt put his hand flat on the door and pushed it to.

The latch went in and caught with a click, which was the most ordinary sound anybody had heard in this building, and the room went back to having no sides.

They picked up their bags.

"Is the car locked," Hank said.

"Yes," Alex said.

"You didn't check it."

"It's locked, Hank."

"Because if it's out there unlocked for—"

"It's locked."

Somebody laughed, one syllable, not a good one, and then nobody did.

---

Alex made her do it again.

"No," Stacy said.

"I want a number. If we ever come back here at a run, I want to know what we've got." The notebook was open on the counter and the pen was in her left hand, because the right one was taped. "You hum, I count."

So she stood in front of a door in an art supply store and hummed a tune she could not name, and the brass came up solid, and the tall painter opened it onto the afternoon. Alex counted out loud with her thumb against her own wrist. Burt held the door. Nobody stood in the gap.

At twenty-two the warm air thinned. At twenty-six the white lines went flat, the way a photograph of a car park is flat. At about thirty the brass stopped having a top and a bottom, the frame closed down to a line, and all of outside became a picture of outside, and then stopped being that too. Burt's hand came off five panels of oak.

"Thirty," Alex said. "Thirty, near enough."

She wrote it down, on the same page as the other things — down is an escalator, up is stairs, don't touch anything — a list a person keeps when they have stopped expecting to leave soon. "That's for after," she said, and closed the notebook.

Thirty seconds. Stacy did not say the thing she had noticed about thirty seconds, because saying it would have made everybody stand still, and over their heads the piano was already doing its climb and its fall and its small stop while Alex put the pen away.

---

She thanked them.

They were at the next wall by then, a wheeled tray between them, in front of a stretch where a forest went from trees to pencil and stopped. The lead painter said *aye* without turning round, and the short one lifted a hand.

Stacy did not ask them about Jordan. She had asked everybody in this building about Jordan — in shops, on ice, across a fountain — and she stood there and thanked four people and asked them nothing at all, and could not have said which of them she was sparing.

Alex was out by the pigment bins saying something about the kiosk in the wall, the one in the plaza that wasn't a shop.

Stacy came out last.

At the back of the shop the wall had a door on it. Five panels, oak, three hinges, a keyhole with a bottom in it, and a hole at the height of a person's eye, and past the hole a car park in flat white lines that any one of them could have been standing in.

The handle was a brass shine on a wall, and under the handle was a small painted shadow, laid on by somebody who cared, falling the way no light in this building had ever fallen.

She stopped in the doorway and looked at it. Then she went out into the light with no source, and she was the last one to stop looking.
