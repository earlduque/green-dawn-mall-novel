## 13 · Employee of the Month

> *GREEN DAWN REWARDS. Membership is free with any qualifying purchase. Members enjoy access to all Levels. Membership cannot be transferred, refused, or removed. Welcome to the family!*

Hank had the man on tape inside six seconds, because the man behind the desk was the worst thing on this floor and he wanted a record of it.

Nothing was the matter with him. That was the whole of it. He was somewhere past fifty, in a jacket the colour of the wall behind him, both hands flat on the counter, and his face was already doing the thing a face does when a car turns into the driveway. Hank had shot a lot of people being glad to see somebody. It comes out one way when it's a routine and another way when it isn't, and this was the second one, aimed at five strangers.

"Oh," the man said. "Oh, look at this. Five of you."

Hank had not filmed the parking lot.

He had stood eight feet from it with the camera on his shoulder and the tape running. Real light had come through that opening — light with a place it came from, laying a hard-edged shadow across the floor — and he had got the painters, and the drill going into the plaster, and he had not raised the camera at the doorway. Not at the cars. Not at the white lines. Nobody had asked him about it.

Batteries, he had decided, on the walk across. He had two spares and the case.

So it wasn't the batteries.

"Is it Level Five you're after?" the man said. "Because that's right there. Right behind me."

He turned and pointed with two fingers, the way you point out a fire exit to a room full of people.

Behind him the wall was a wall. Every other surface in the Unfinished Gallery was halfway into being something — a treeline going to pencil, a face with no face in it yet — and the panel behind the Servants' Desk was primed and grey and had never had a brush on it. Nothing to take a shot of.

Hank put a shot of it on the tape anyway, four seconds, held.

"That's a wall," Burt said.

"That's Level Five," the man said, delighted about it.

---

His counter had a bell on it, a pen on a chain, and a rack of brochures. His badge had a sun on it and no name.

The requirement was a loyalty card. He said it the way a person tells you the parking is validated, and Alex asked the obvious question with no weight on it at all.

"How do we get one?"

"Oh, that's easy." He straightened up. "You stay with us at Green Dawn Mall for four hundred and twenty-two years."

Under the ventilation the piano got to the bottom of its little fall and started again from the top.

"Four hundred and twenty-two," Burt said.

"Four hundred and twenty-two."

"Years."

"Years."

Nobody laughed. Stacy smiled and it went no further up her face than her mouth. Djan looked at the counter. Alex said, "Okay," in the voice she used for writing something down, and Hank found the camera down at his hip, still running, pointed at the man's belt buckle, which was not a shot.

Then the man's whole face opened up.

"Oh — *or*," he said. "Or you shop with us. You shop at Green Dawn Mall and you procure goods, and then you've got one." He looked round the five of them like a man handing out sweets. "Any qualifying purchase."

"Shopping," Stacy said. "See, now that I can do."

---

Alex took a brochure off the rack while the man was telling Burt how nice it was to have people in.

The cover said GREEN DAWN MALL, and under that a green sun with eight rays, and nothing else — no year, no address, no phone. Inside was a directory, and Hank read it over her shoulder the way he read a shot list, all at once first and then again in order.

Level One: Television Libertés. Magic Eye. A photo studio. A food counter, struck out with one ruled line of pen. Level Two: PageTurn, Discworld, Tangled Time, and two more lines gone the same way. Level Three ran longest and had four strikes together in the middle of it, and Hank thought about the corridor of boarded glass they had walked past on the way to the ice, and did not say so. Level Four had five names and all five stood.

Then Level Five, in the same type as the others, with the little sun beside it, and nothing under it. Not a strike, not a note. The paper went on being white down to the fold. Hank had seen a board like that once, white letters in black felt, screwed to a wall on the fourth floor of an office building.

Alex folded the brochure along its own crease and put it in her coat pocket, and did not open it again, and said, "There's a souvenir place."

"There's a souvenir place," Burt agreed, and hitched the pack. "All right, team. We're going shopping."

---

Things We Keep was one room, and everything in it said GREEN DAWN MALL.

Snow globes on a glass shelf, the mall inside them with a fountain the size of a pea. A pegboard of keychains. A bin of rubber magnets, pencils in a jar, shot glasses, spoons in a felt tray, pennants in the green, mugs, T-shirts folded in one size only. A spinner rack of postcards by the door with a squeak in the bearing.

There was no price on anything. Hank checked, because he had already picked out what he wanted.

In the middle of the floor a kid sat cross-legged with a stack of cards at one knee.

Eight, maybe nine. Sneakers, a shirt with nothing written on it, four piles started on the carpet with no rule in them that Hank could find. He did not look up when five adults came in under the chime, which Hank had seen kids do a thousand times, in a thousand places where the parents were.

"Excuse me," Djan said. "Can we buy something?"

The kid was standing in front of Djan.

There was no part where he crossed the floor. He had been on the carpet with his legs folded and then he was up at Djan's chest height, close enough that Djan had to move his elbow, with one hand out flat.

"Help me!"

It was not a child's voice. It came out at a child's volume and a child's pitch with something a great deal older sitting underneath it, and the worst of it was how cheerful it was.

Nobody moved for a second. Then Burt said, "Well, sure," and got down on one knee with a grunt, and that was that.

Alex tried the other way first, because Alex always tried the other way first. She lifted a keychain off the pegboard with the taped hand and walked it toward the door, and then she was at the pegboard with the keychain back on its hook and her hand coming away from it. She looked at the door, and at the hook, and said, "All right."

---

The top of the stack was paper.

Ordinary card, printed on one side, blank on the back. Stacy took them by the corner and passed them over and the kid put each one down on whichever pile he put it on, fast, certain, and if you asked about the piles he said *that one goes there*, so after a while nobody asked. Burt got the paper sack out and offered it to him. The kid said he wasn't hungry. Burt put the sack away.

Around the middle of the stack the edges started holding a line.

Hank got the first one. He was turning a card to hand off and the edge went into the knuckle of his index finger, and there was a second where it was nothing and then it was a lot. One clean opening, an inch long, no ragged part to it. He got his thumb round the talisman in his pocket and squeezed, which had never once been about the talisman, and said a short word.

The blood went on the card. The kid took it and put it in a pile. Burt taped the knuckle without being asked and without stopping what he was doing.

Then it got worse. Alex pulled two postcards off the rack and used them as tongs, which was a good idea for about a second and a half; the card came up between them and went through both at once, and four halves lay on the carpet of a shop where nothing was allowed to leave. Hank tried the flat driver out of his kit as a lever, four angles, and the card did not lift and did not slide and did not do anything at all. Djan had one nearly clear when it turned in his fingers and dropped, and it went into the carpet edge-first and stood up out of the nap like a fin, and it was still standing there when they finished. Nobody got it out. The kid worked around it.

Then Burt said, "Hank. Give me that little driver."

---

He did it kneeling, with the roll of gaffer tape in his left hand, talking the whole time and not to anybody.

He pulled off four inches, and the sound of gaffer tape off a roll was one Hank had recorded on purpose, for the edit. Burt folded the strip back on itself and pressed the two ends together so the length of it went inside out — sticky on the outside, all the way round, a loop the size of a wedding ring with the tack facing the world. Then he wound a shorter piece round the shank of the screwdriver, seated the loop on the tip, wrapped again, and squeezed the join between two fingers until it set.

"Now," he said, and lowered it onto the middle of the top card.

The card came up off the stack by its face.

It hung there off the end of a two-dollar screwdriver, dead level, clear air under every edge of it, and Burt turned his wrist and set it on the pile and pressed the middle with the tip, and the card let go and stayed.

"There she is," Burt said.

Hank had the camera on his shoulder. The light was flat and coming out of nothing, the way it always was, which meant no setup and nothing to fix. It was the best thing he had seen in this building — a man in a scuffed hard hat, down on one knee on the carpet of a souvenir shop, working a stack of blades with a bit of tape folded backwards.

He did not raise it.

Burt made a second crane. He made it slower than he had to, holding each fold up where the kid could see it, saying *fold her back on herself, see, so she's got no clean side*, and then he put the screwdriver in the kid's hand and closed the fingers round it the way you hand a tool to somebody you intend to keep working with.

The kid got it on the first try, made a noise Hank had heard in a hundred driveways, and did not stop grinning for the rest of it.

After that it went fast. Two cranes, two people, a rhythm — lift, turn, set, press — and Burt calling the piles like a man calling a load off a truck, and the kid answering, *that one goes there,* and the stack going down. Stacy sat on the floor. Djan sat on the floor. Alex stood with the taped hand on the rack and watched the door.

Near the bottom Hank saw a picture side going past. The Atrium fountain, in colour, water in the basin, and eight or nine people standing round the rim with their backs to whoever took it. He handed it over and Burt lifted it and the kid put it where it went.

The last card came off the stack on the kid's crane. It was thin enough that Hank could see light through it, and it went down flat onto the pile, and then the kid pinched it off the tape with his own fingers and pressed it square with his thumb, and nothing happened to his hand.

"Well," Burt said, getting up in stages. "Good crew."

The kid was behind the register in an apron.

---

The register was mechanical, with a drawer and a bell in it, and the kid stood on something behind the counter to work it.

Hank went straight up. He had known which magnet since the first ten seconds in the room: rubber, three inches, the green sun with the eight rays, GREEN DAWN MALL in a script across the bottom.

"Boy," Hank said, "I want to buy this."

He pointed at it.

Behind him, four people looked at him. He could feel it on the back of his neck the way you feel a light being turned on. Stacy said his name in one syllable.

The kid did not mind at all. He rang it in.

Burt put a mall token on the counter and the kid pushed it back with one finger and said they didn't take those. So Alex got the roll out of her coat — a paper tube of quarters with the end torn open, and loose change with it, the same roll she had walked in with — and counted it onto the counter with the taped hand, and paid for everybody, and nobody made her offer twice.

Four postcards and a magnet.

They chose them off the squeaking rack while the drawer was open. Burt took the Ice Rink, because it was a nice rink. Djan took a corridor with nothing in it. Alex went round the rack twice and took the fountain. Stacy took the one of the sign out on the road with the letters still on it, black on white and readable, and Hank thought about that sign as it had been when they walked past it, bleached years ago to the shape of where the words had been, and said nothing. Stacy was still looking at him.

The drawer went shut with the bell.

"Thanks for helping me sort things," the kid said. "I'll even give you my employee discount."

Alex said that wasn't necessary. The kid did it anyway, worked it out on the register in a second and a half, and gave her back a handful of quarters, and she looked at them in her palm before they went in her pocket.

On the wall behind him, at adult eye height, there was a placard. Brass-coloured plastic in a wooden frame, a hook under it for the photograph that wasn't in it, and words engraved across the top.

EMPLOYEE OF THE MONTH.

The name below it was *Boi.*

B, O, I. Cut into the plastic with the rest of it, in the same type, spaced out the way engravers space letters when they have the room.

Nobody said anything for a second.

"Well," Burt said. "Nice work, Boi."

"Thank you," Boi said.

---

Before they went, he opened the drawer again — the bell, the rattle of the till on its rails — and took five cards from the section at the back where a register keeps stamps.

They were stickers.

Small rectangles, glossy, the green sun in the middle, GREEN DAWN REWARDS along the bottom in white. Each on a square of waxed backing with a split down the middle, the way a sticker comes so you can get a thumbnail under the edge and start it.

He handed them out over the counter, one each, and got them in the right hands without being told which of them was which.

"Now you're members," Boi said.

Nobody argued. Stacy said thank you and meant it, and Burt shook the kid's hand and the kid shook back, and Djan looked at the sorted piles for longer than they were worth, and Alex was already turning for the door and the desk and the wall behind it.

They came out under the chime into the paint smell and the light with no place it came from, five of them, holding small green cards by the edges, and not one of the five had peeled the backing off yet.
