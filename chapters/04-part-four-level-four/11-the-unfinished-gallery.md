## 11 · The Unfinished Gallery
### *The Hanged Man*

> *UNTITLED (in progress). Artist unknown. Medium: mixed. This work is being completed on site. Patrons are asked not to look away.*

Djan was three steps below everybody, with his hand on the rail, and he knew this one was wrong before he had gone the length of a person down it.

The door had said EXIT. It had opened onto carpet, a bench, a bin with a fresh liner, and a corridor with an escalator at the end. Burt had held it with his boot and counted them through underneath the sign, and Djan watched his face on the way past — a face doing arithmetic it already had the answer to — and did not take the photograph, and the not-taking cost him something he did not intend to itemize.

Three floors, a camera, and nothing. A wall in the Atrium. A counter with two pits in the carpet. The outside of the thing, every time. So: one frame off this floor that held what the floor was. He made that arrangement between the second step and the third, which was the last moment on that escalator when he could make an arrangement about anything.

---

The seam of the wall beside him was travelling. Not rising past him at the speed of the stair. Travelling left. He put his palm flat on it: straight, cool, still going left under his hand while his feet went down. The handrail ran to the bottom in one line, and the bottom sat off to the right of where the line pointed by about a shop front.

"There's a song in this," Burt said, above him. "In the step. Coming up through the sole. There's a tune in it and I know it."

He hummed four notes. It was not the Jingle. The Jingle was up in the ceiling doing the little climb and the little fall, louder here than on the ice, and the four notes went across the top of it and did not belong to it. "I can't get the name of it," he said, and said nothing else the rest of the way down.

The panels were pulsing. Djan had it a while before he was willing to have it: the whole wall going out about an inch and coming back, slow, all of it together, the join lines opening and closing like a thing being worked.

"That's a heartbeat," Alex said. She was two steps above Burt with the taped hand against her chest. "In the wall. Two beats. Listen — there. *Toot-toot.*"

And then it was there.

His whole trade had been pointed at that wall and there had been no sound in it, and Alex said what it was and the wall began doing what she said. Two beats and a rest, under the ventilation, under the piano, and all five of them turned their heads at once in the same direction.

Then he got the camera up and took a frame, knowing before the shutter closed he would get a wall. Grey panel, join line, light with no lamp in it. Nothing in his kit had ever taken a picture of what a room was doing to the people in it. He had known that since he was nineteen and it had never once been a grief until this building.

The turpentine got to him a good second before the light did.

---

Level Four was wide and it was full of paint.

He came off the bottom step into linseed and turps and the smell raw pigment has before anybody puts oil in it. The ceiling was one white surface, no grid, no vent, giving off even light out of nothing.

Every wall in the plaza was mid-mural. A cityscape, towers and a bridge and windows worked in one at a time, running west until the paint thinned and became bare canvas. A forest where the near trunks were oil and the trees behind them were pencil. A portrait twelve feet high, a coat with every button on it, and where the face went, primer and two guide lines crossed at the middle, waiting.

Brushes hung in the air beside the work at the height a hand would hold them. Some were still. Some painted: in to the wall, a stroke, back out, hanging there loaded and dripping, and the drips ran down the mural in globs and dried where they landed.

Djan counted windows in the near tower, because counting was the one instrument he had brought that this room could not confiscate.

Eleven.

He looked at his shoes. He looked back up.

Twelve. The new one was on the fourth floor, third from the left, and it had a light on in it. He counted again, and looked away for the length of a breath, and came back, and a window on the second floor had gone dark and there was a bridge cable that had not been there.

Hank had a piece of a cardboard box up off the floor. He put the corner of it flat on the forest at knee height and dragged it a foot sideways, and the green came away in a smear and the wall underneath went a dirty white.

"That's wet," Hank said. "That's wet like an hour wet."

"Get back off it," Burt said.

They got back off it, and Djan watched, because he was the one who would. It took four seconds. Green came up out of the wall underneath and filled the drag mark and closed it, and the trunk was there again, and it had bark on it now — two ridges and a knot with the grain turning round it. Better than before Hank wiped it off.

"Okay," Stacy said, in a small voice, and put her hands into her handbag and left them there.

---

Two corridors and five storefronts, and Djan had the floor in his head in one circuit, which was the last thing on Level Four that went the way he expected. One corridor was short and empty — bench, bin, two units papered over — a place a person could sit down. The other had the escalator down at the end.

Then the frontages: **Canvas & Craft**, art materials; **Still Life**, frames and prints; **Darkened Glass**, second-hand, the stock stacked face-in so you saw only the backs of the boards; **Things We Keep**, souvenirs. And between two units, not a shop at all, a kiosk with a brass bell on the counter, and a man in a waistcoat behind it who lifted a hand at Djan across sixty feet of plaza. The sign said **the Servants' Desk**.

Djan lifted a hand back and kept walking.

---

The easel stood in the middle of the floor and it was an easel — three legs of pine, a tray with two brushes, a canvas about the size of a card table. The paint on it was finished. It was the only finished thing on the level.

A room made entirely of screens. Floor, walls, ceiling, corners, all of it lit surface, all of it showing something too small to read, and the light coming off all of it at once so the middle of the room was a glare. Back in the glare, small, half taken out by it, a figure with one arm raised.

He gave it the front for a long time. Then he walked round it.

The back of the canvas was the painting.

Not reversed. The same room, the same glare, the same figure with the same arm up — and he was behind the figure now, and had been in front of it a second ago, and was still in front of it, because it was the same painting and he could see it.

He took the front and held it, the way he did with a frame he meant to keep, and walked the four steps and took the back, and the front went. Not faded. Went, the way a word goes when you have said it eight times. He came back round and the back went.

He got the reflector umbrella off his shoulder and set the silver so the back of the canvas came into the fabric while his eyes had the front. Whichever one he had, the other was a fact he knew and could not see, and the moment he leaned on that, the first became the fact.

Both eyes were watering the way they do in wind, and he had lost some amount of time he could not account for, and Alex was saying his name in the voice she used when she had already said it.

Nothing had ever failed to come apart for him. You stand in front of a thing long enough and it becomes its parts, and you take the parts. This did not come apart. It sat there being both, and let him fail at it, and had no opinion about him at all.

"Anything?" Alex said.

"It's the same on both sides," Djan said, and closed the umbrella and put it back on his shoulder.

---

"Hold on," Hank said.

He came in with the Betacam down against his chest and racked the lens out. "Look at this. Look *through* it, don't look at it."

Djan took the camera. The eyepiece was a monochrome cup the size of a matchbox, and the frame came up with the edges gone and the glare knocked down, and it was one side of the painting, because one side is all a lens can carry.

It held still.

Hair gone light in that glare. Glasses with two rectangles of screen sitting in them. The right arm up and slightly out, elbow high, wrist turned over, and a camera in the hand — not held up to see over anybody, but at the angle a person holds it when it is running and they want the whole room in it.

Around and under and above that: screens. No desk. No chair. No door.

"It's Jordan," Djan said.

Stacy took the camera off him with both hands and got her eye in and went very quiet, and then she said, "He needs a haircut."

Nobody laughed. Burt took his turn, and then Alex, a long time, with the taped hand up against the cup to keep the light out.

"That the room from upstairs?" Burt said. "First floor. All the screens, the lady in the hat."

"No."

"It's screens."

"That room had a desk. It had a chair with the cushion flat on one side. Every screen in it had a frame round it and a number in the corner." Djan put a finger an inch off the canvas. "There's no frames in this. There's no seam in it anywhere. The floor's screens and the ceiling's screens and there's nothing in it a person could sit on."

"So it's a different room," Alex said.

"It's a different room."

"How many rooms in this building have got all the screens in them," she said, and nobody had anything for that.

"That's the lens," Hank said. "You couldn't get it because you were looking with your eyes. It's a two-sided thing and a lens only takes one side."

"That's the lens," Djan said.

He did not say that he had been beaten by a canvas in a shopping mall, flatly, the first time in his working life, and that a man with no patience had solved it in nine seconds by accident. He let the tape have it. Hank was explaining the principle to Burt, who was nodding, and it was not a correct principle, and Djan let him.

Jordan was alive down here. That was the first thing they had that was not three days old.

---

Stacy laid cards on the floor, one for each frontage, and asked everybody to be quiet, and everybody was.

The first spread came out pages. Four of them, one after another.

"That's not a read," she said.

She gathered them and shuffled, her thumb going fast along the edge of the deck, and laid five again, and got pages again. Different pages. Nothing pointing at any door on this level.

"Well," she said, brightly, "they're all *lovely*," and had the deck squared and back in the bag before anyone could look at it properly, and stood and smoothed the front of her skirt twice. She was good at that, the way a person is good at something they have had to be good at.

Then she stopped with her hand still on her skirt. "My phone played music at me. Ten seconds of it." She got down again, and did not shuffle, and turned one card over, and did not look at it. "Shush."

Djan stood in the middle of Level Four with the piano in the ceiling and the ventilation and the two-beat in the walls, and listened as hard as he had ever listened to anything, and heard the piano in the ceiling and the ventilation and the two-beat in the walls.

Stacy pointed at Canvas & Craft. "There. It's a tune. Don't talk to me, it's coming out of *there*."

He followed her, which was the second time on this floor he had taken somebody else's word for what the room was doing, and he did not care for the arithmetic of that at all.

---

Canvas & Craft was deep and it was stocked. Rolls of canvas on end in a barrel. Bins of dry pigment with a scoop in each — ultramarine, ochre, bone, a green with the card gone blank. A pegboard hung with brushes, hundreds of them, graded from a housepainter's four-inch down to one with a head like a single eyelash. And the whole right side of the store was yarn, floor to ceiling in pigeonholes, with its own side room behind it.

Alex stopped at the yarn. She put the taped hand out toward it and did not touch it, and stood there a second longer than she needed to.

At the back of the shop, four figures in paint-stained aprons were working on a door.

The door was painted on the wall. Djan came in to two feet and it was paint — brush marks, a ridge where two loads had met, the grain of the plaster coming through. At four feet it was a door: six panels, a brass handle with an escutcheon that had a keyhole in it, and a line of dark down the hinge side where a door stands a sixteenth of an inch off its frame. One of them was on his knees at the bottom with a brush the width of a fingernail, putting in the shadow underneath.

The four worked together. Not in turn — together, four brushes going to the wall and coming off it at the same moment, no word between them, not one of them ever in another one's way. Djan had spent twenty years finding out who was in charge of a room by watching hands. He watched these and could not tell.

"You've got no extraction in here," Burt said. "Four fellas and that much thinner and there's not a fan in this room."

"I'd love to help you," said the one at the top of the door, without stopping, without turning, in a Scottish accent. "We're busy right now."

Stacy went at it three ways. Blonde, glasses, an old camera he wouldn't put down, would have been asking you questions — that was her best one, and it was true, and it was the sort of thing that opens a person. Then: had anybody come through going down. Then, quieter, a minute was all they needed.

"There'll be somebody who knows," the lead one said, pleasantly, and laid a highlight along the top of the brass. "We're on the door."

They were not rude. Djan had been shut down by every kind of person there is and this was a new one — not unwilling, not available, no crack in it to get a thumbnail into. What those four wanted was to finish that door and find out whether it opened, and beside that a woman asking after a missing kid was weather.

Stacy stood holding a question with nowhere to put it. She had never once, in front of Djan, run out of a way to ask.

"We'll wait," Alex said.

---

So five adults stood in an art supply store while four people finished a door.

It took about five minutes. Burt got the paper sack out and offered it round and put it away again. Hank filmed the pegboard, and then the painters, and got bored, and changed a battery he did not need to change. Stacy took her weight off one heel and then the other, because nobody in this crew sat on anything any more.

Djan watched the hands. Then he set himself at eleven feet, waited for the four brushes to come in to the wall together, and took the photograph. Whatever else this building did to his film, four people finishing a door was four people finishing a door, and it was the first thing since the lift that had held still long enough to be worth a frame.

He wound on.

The painters stepped back off the wall at the same time.

They stood in a row and looked at it, and one of them let out a breath, and there was nothing in any of them but gladness. A hand came up and rested a second on somebody's shoulder.

Then the lead one laid his brush across the tray, and wiped two fingers on his apron, and turned around, and looked directly at the five of them — the first time on Level Four that anybody had.

"It's missing something."
