## 10 · Fault

> *IN THE EVENT OF FIRE, do not use the escalators. Proceed to the nearest door marked EXIT. All doors marked EXIT lead to another part of Green Dawn Mall.*

Every visor on that ice was pointed at him, so Burt Calloway put his crew to work.

"Hank. Walk it in, don't run it in, you'll go down. Djan, get on that door we came through, back to it, eyes on me. Alex, off the boards." Alex was leaning on the boards. "Off. That's a rail somebody else owns."

They did the things, the way a crew does when the man in the hat is talking and the alternative is standing still. Two dozen people looking at you at once means a load coming down or a thing you'll shortly have to fix, and either way everybody should be holding a job when it arrives.

He counted them. Five, counting the one out on the ice, and he counted him.

Hank came in off the ice with the handbag held out in front of him at arm's length, which for Hank was an enormous amount of respect for an object.

"It's heavy," he said.

"Set it on the counter."

---

There was a skate counter along the left wall, laminate top, a mat behind it worn through where somebody used to stand. Sixty-odd pairs of rental skates in the cubbies with the laces tucked. One pair, third row, had water standing on the blades.

He set the bag down and did not reach in. He tipped it and let the thing come out onto the laminate on its own.

It was a big hand.

Bigger than Burt's, and Burt's had been the biggest hand in most rooms he'd stood in. Frost in the hairs on the back of it, white at the knuckles, grey underneath. The wrist end was frosted over thick and Burt looked at that once. You could feel the cold off it a foot away, the way you feel a chest freezer with the lid up.

And it had a drill in it.

"Oh, that's not right," Burt said.

"Yeah," Stacy said, from up on the mat. "That's what I said."

"No. *That.*" He put a knuckle near it, not on it. "There's no guard. There's no lock on that button. That's a live tool sitting in a fist where anybody could knock it, and the bit's out, and there's no cap on the bit."

Alex looked at him for a second. "Burt."

"I'm saying what it is."

He was aware there was a severed hand on the counter of a skate rental. He had that filed. But a hand on a counter had already happened to somebody, and the drill was an accident that hadn't happened yet, and Burt's trade was the second kind.

He got the flashlight off Djan and put it on the fingers.

"Alright," he said. "Somebody say it."

"Say what," Hank said.

"Say whose it isn't."

---

They did it out loud and fast, which was the point. A man who is listing reasons is not a man standing there.

"It's a left," Djan said.

"Go on."

"The tape says the camcorder's in his right hand. If this building was taking one off him it'd take that one."

"That's a guess," Alex said.

"It's a left hand," Djan said.

"Size," Stacy said. "Look at it next to Alex."

Alex put her taped hand flat on the counter beside it and there was no argument to be had. You could have got both of Jordan's inside that. Jordan carried a mug in two hands.

"And there's no mole," Djan said.

Everybody looked at him.

"Back of the hand, under the little finger, about here." He didn't take his own hands out of his pockets to show them; he moved his chin instead. "Brown, size of a match head. He's got one on both."

"You never said that before," Alex said.

"Nobody ever asked me what his hands looked like."

Burt ran the flashlight under the little finger and there was nothing there. Skin, frost, an old scar on the second joint that was somebody's.

"Okay," Alex said, and let her breath out, and it went out in the cold as a white cloud. "Okay. It's not him."

Burt stood over the counter with the flashlight still on and worked out that the relief had gone bad on him in about four seconds flat.

Because it was somebody's. A person who was not their person had a hand missing, it had been out on that ice getting swept in front of, and nobody in this room thought that worth bringing up. Stacy had said hello to them. They had said hey.

Behind the counter there was a cardboard box with LOST + FOUND on the side in marker, and in it were a child's mitten, one earring, and a wallet with nothing in it. On a site you had a place for this. A ring came off in the mud and it went in the tin on the office windowsill and sat there till the man came back for it. The thing on the counter would not fit in that box, and there was nobody to hand it to.

He filed that where he filed things.

"Right," he said. "Stacy. You said you had an idea."

---

"I'm not snapping them," Stacy said.

"I didn't say snap them."

"Hank said snap them."

"I said *bend*," Hank said.

"You made a noise with your mouth. You went *crk*."

The fingers were frozen around the drill, and frozen things go one way when you pull on them. Burt had been braced for that sound.

Stacy went into the Neverfull past the makeup bag and came out with a curling iron.

There was an outlet behind the counter, at the back of the kick space, painted over at the edges. She got down on the mat in that skirt and put the plug in, and the little light on the barrel came on orange, and something in Burt's chest went sideways, because there was power in the wall behind a rink at the bottom of a building nobody could find the bottom of.

"It takes a minute," she said.

"Take the minute."

He got the paper sack out and offered it round. Djan took one, looked at it, and put it in his jacket pocket, which was the furthest that sack had got with anybody since the fourth floor.

Somewhere under the boards a compressor hummed up through the mat, the only thing in the building doing an honest job. Over the top of it the piano came round, the little climb and the little fall, half a beat behind itself off the ice.

Stacy tested the iron against the back of her wrist the way you test an iron. Then she knelt up to the counter, took the hand at the base of the thumb through her sleeve so as not to have skin on it, and laid the barrel across the middle two fingers.

It hissed.

"Okay," she said, not to anybody. "Okay. There you go."

Water came off it and ran across the laminate and dripped off the front edge onto the mat, one drop and then another and then a run of them. She held the iron where it was and did not push. Every so often she moved it a quarter inch and waited. The smell was hot metal, and under that something else Burt didn't go looking for a word for.

It took a good while. Nobody hurried her. Hank had the camera down at his hip. Djan had turned round to watch and hadn't raised his own, which Burt noticed.

"I'm sorry," Stacy said, once, quietly, into the counter. "This is going to be quick."

And Burt stood in a cold room holding a flashlight on a dead man's hand while a woman in heels warmed the fingers open one at a time because she had decided that snapping them would be rude, and he thought, in a plain and uncomplicated way, that he would like to know who raised her.

The first finger came up off the housing. Then the second, slower, and the whole hand shifted and opened out on the counter like something letting go of a rope.

They did not break. Burt had been listening for it and the sound didn't come.

---

The drill sat there on the laminate on its own, wet, steaming faintly. Both of Burt's hands would have covered it. Grey housing, black grip, one button, one thick bit, and along the side in raised letters with the paint still down in them: ICE DRILLER 3000.

"Well, there you go," Burt said. "It's a drill."

"It's a drill for ice," Hank said.

"That's what it says."

Alex picked it up with the taped hand, three fingers and a thumb, and held it out at the length of her arm.

"Hank," she said. "Don't use your finger."

Hank unrolled the screwdriver kit, picked one, leaned in from the side, and pressed the button.

It roared.

Burt had run a breaker saw for eleven years and this was louder. It came back off the ice and the roof and the boards from three directions at once, out of all proportion to a thing you could hold in one hand.

And Alex's arm did not move.

Burt was watching for that, because that was what he watched. A tool that noisy should have been walking her wrist around and pulling at the elbow. No shake, no wander — the bit turning true at the end of her arm like it was bolted to the building.

Hank pressed the button again and the room came back. Out on the ice not one of them had moved. Two dozen visors, all pointed this way, the sweepers still with their brooms up.

"Alex," Stacy said. "How do you feel."

"Fine."

"Alex."

"I feel fine, Stacy."

It had not done anything to her. That was the plain fact of it, and it sat in the middle of them, and nobody would have it. Hank got the camera up on her face. Djan watched her hands. Burt counted a slow minute, and at the end of it Alex was standing there holding a drill and looking irritated.

"Put it down," Burt said.

She put it down.

"Pick it up."

She picked it up.

"Now put it down again."

She put it down again, immediately, no argument in her face, and that was the whole test and it came back clean. Nobody in this crew had been able to put anything down all day. She could put it down.

"It's a tool," he said.

"Everything here is a something," Alex said.

"It's a *tool*." He picked it up himself, bare-handed, because somebody had to and he was the one wearing the hat, and it sat in his palm balanced so well he turned it over twice looking for the reason. Best-made object he'd touched since he walked into that lift. It had come out of a dead man's fist on an ice rink and it did nothing to him at all. "Off. Keep it off. There's no charger in this place I'd put my name to. Stacy, bag."

Stacy held the Neverfull open and he laid it in on top of the deck.

"It goes in there till there's a job for it," he said. "There'll be a job for it."

---

Three across — Alex, Hank, Djan, tied on at the waist — and Burt on the other end of the sixty feet with his boots set against the door frame. Stacy at the entrance with the bag, in the heels, not going anywhere and open about it.

Two on this side and three on that side is not five. He did it anyway, because the rope was a load, and a load he could feel in his hands was worth more than anything he could shout across a room.

The hand went with them.

"It's somebody's," he said, when Hank looked at him about it. "It ain't staying on a counter."

Alex carried it in the crook of her taped arm, and the front of her coat went dark where it had started to thaw.

Burt paid the rope hand over hand and watched three sets of feet and waited for the thing to happen, because on the last two floors it had happened. The steps had got longer. The car had started moving. He stood there waiting to take a weight.

Nothing happened.

They got to the middle of the ice and nothing happened. Djan had the reflector umbrella up on his shoulder, closed, held like a spear, a decision he'd made privately that nobody had talked him out of. Alex walked like a woman crossing a street.

They stepped up onto the rubber lip at the far boards and nothing happened.

Under the scoreboard at that end there was a door. White, wide, a push bar across it, the paint gone at knee height where trolleys had hit it for years. Above it, a sign — green ground, white letters, lit from behind.

`EXIT`

Burt read it twice. He had read a notice on a wall twice, days ago or weeks ago, up in a lobby with a sawhorse in it, and he hadn't said anything about that one either.

But EXIT meant something. It meant it on every site he'd ever worked. You do not put that word over a door that goes further in; that is not a thing a person does. Somebody had run wire to that sign and set the letters in it, and out past a door like that there'd be white lines and four cars, and one of the four would be theirs.

"That's the way out," he called across.

"That's *a* door," Alex called back.

"That's the way out."

---

Alex got the push bar with her hip, and the door gave, and she stepped off the ice onto carpet.

Every curler on that rink started yelling at once.

Burt had two dozen silent people in his eyeline and then he had two dozen people coming — brooms up, boots digging, coaches and all, a noise out of them like a yard at shift end.

The three at the far door had their backs to it. That was the part. The room was coming at them and they hadn't heard it, because it had spent all this time making no sound at all, and the ear takes a second to believe that.

"ALEX—" Stacy screamed. "ALEX, BEHIND YOU, BEHIND—"

"HEY!" Burt hollered, in the voice, the one that came up out of the floor. "NO RUNNING ON MY CONSTRUCTION SITE!"

It did not slow them by one step. He noted that, and intended to have a think about it later, because it had never once failed before.

Then Alex turned, and got the hand up out of her arm, and whipped it back onto the ice as she ran.

It was a terrible throw. She was going the wrong way, she had it in the taped hand, she was throwing left across her own body, and it came off her fingers low and wrong and Burt's whole chest went with it. It hit the ice flat and kept going — the length of that rink on its own back, the two thawed fingers up and flailing loose — and came to rest a yard off where a stone would sit.

Everything stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped, mid-stride, the whole floor of them, brooms still up. Then one of the sweepers went back and picked it up in two gloved hands and carried it in to the middle, and the rest fell in behind him, the way people do when the shift is on again.

Djan lowered the umbrella off his shoulder.

"Around," Stacy said, and had Burt by the sleeve. "Around, we're going around, I am *not* walking on that."

She took him round the outside of the boards at a clip that would have been a good pace for a man in flat shoes, past the benches and a bin of split brooms, and Burt went at the pace she set because she had hold of his sleeve.

They came up to the others at the far end.

Five. Nobody hurt, nothing broken, nobody's hands on anything they shouldn't have been. No fight anywhere in it. They had taken a thing that belonged to somebody else and given it back, and Burt would have liked that written down somewhere, because it was the first problem in this building that came apart in his hands the honest way.

Behind them, out on the ice, a voice went up across the whole rink — the first raised voice in that room that wasn't his.

"I'M COUNTING THAT AS A FAULT!"

And above the door the board changed. Not both of the numbers. One of them, quiet about it, one figure going over the way they do, and underneath it the long count went on running in a number that was not any amount of time.

The door stood open under the sign, with Hank's boot in it.

Burt got the pack up on both shoulders, and counted them off — five — and walked toward it.
