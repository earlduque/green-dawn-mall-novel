## 09 · Ninety-Nine Thousand

> *GREEN DAWN ICE — RINK RULES. 1. No food on the ice. 2. No street shoes. 3. Return the stone to the team that threw it. 4. The clock does not stop. 5. Losing is permitted.*

"Alex. Get that door and hold it."

Alex got the door and held it. It was a heavy door and it took her shoulder and her hip and the taped hand flat against the glass, and she had it open and braced before she had finished hearing the end of the sentence.

That was the trouble. She had spent her whole working life asking the people who gave her instructions what they thought entitled them to, and the part of her that did that had not put its hand up. It had gone along. It had been glad to go along. Burt came through under her arm with the backpack and the hard hat and said, "Good," and something behind her ribs sat up like a dog at a door.

She let the other three by and went in last, and made a note of it. Not of the door. Of the pleasure.

Then the cold got her.

It was on the other side of the doors in a slab and she walked into the slab. It came up her sleeves first. Her eyes filled and she blinked them clear and they filled again. The inside of her nose shut. The gaffer tape on her right hand, which had been warm since PageTurn and had stopped being a separate thing from the hand, went hard, and she could feel the edge of every wrap of it. Her breath came out of her in a cloud, indoors, and she stood and watched it go.

It was not a draft. A draft moves. This stayed where it was, being a different temperature, and the doors swung shut behind Djan and kept it in there with them.

The floor was black rubber in interlocking sections, ribbed, damp along the seams, and it smelled like a tire shop with the water left running.

The Jingle had come in with them and it did not carry in the cold. The little climb and the little fall were still going, thin, arriving the way music arrives through a wall.

---

Boards ran round the ice at hip height, painted white, scuffed grey along the bottom where skates had come into them for years. Plexiglass above the boards. Behind the plexiglass, four tiers of tip-up seats in blue plastic, every one of them empty and every one of them down. A skate counter along the left wall with cubbies numbered up to sixty and a rubber stamp sitting beside the register. A concession window with its shutter pulled and a price list taped to the shutter — hot chocolate, pretzel, hot dog, chocolate milk — the prices in tokens.

The lamps over the ice hung in banks of long tubes and not one of them was lit. The light came down anyway, even, the same light as the Atrium and the corridors, and nobody out on the ice had a shadow under them.

Two teams were playing curling.

They were most of the way through something. Green bibs over sweaters on one side, white on the other, and every one of them in a helmet, and every helmet with a visor — smoked, curved, brow to chin, the sort you cannot see into from outside. Matching. Both teams.

One of the green ones was down in the hack, low, out from the line on one boot with a broom held wide in the off hand for balance, and he let go at the right moment and stood up.

Two sweepers went with it. They went bent at the waist, brooms flat, working the ice ahead of the frozen severed hand in short strokes, one on each side of its path. A third came behind them at a walk with his broom up and out of the way. At the far end the skip stood in the painted rings with his broom head down on a spot, and moved the spot eight inches to his left, and the sweepers changed what they were doing without anybody telling them to.

It came into the rings and slowed and stopped, and it was closed. Not curled. Closed around something, the way a fist is a different shape from a hand.

There were ten people out there, counting the two at the boards in tracksuits with clipboards, and Alex counted them twice, and the only sound in the building was the stone going down the ice. A long hiss with a grain in it. A chair dragged across a floor in the next room.

---

The far end of the building had a set of double doors in it, and above the doors, bolted flat to the wall, a scoreboard.

It was the kind with bulbs behind coloured plastic. HOME on the left, AWAY on the right, a window under each.

HOME 0. AWAY 0.

Between them, wider, the window for the clock. A rink clock has four digits in it.

99999.

"Djan," Alex said. "Is there an escalator anywhere on this floor."

"No."

He said it the way people answer a question they have already answered for themselves, and she filed that, and looked back at the doors under the scoreboard. If there was no way down out on the promenade then the way down was through there. And the way to there was across.

Burt had gone off along the boards to a gate set into them, wide, chained shut, with a bay behind it and nothing parked in the bay. He put his arms out and walked the width of it.

"That's tight," he said.

"For what."

"For the machine. You'd have to come at that square, and back it in blind." He looked into the bay a while. "There's no machine."

---

Hank got the camera up on his shoulder and put the lens on the hand.

He was thirty feet from it. He ran the barrel out to the end of its travel, and let it come back, and then took the eyepiece off his face.

"Alex. Come look."

She came and looked into the grey monocle, which she had never liked doing, and there it was. The hand filled the frame. It filled it completely and it was as clear as anything she had ever seen on that tape — the frost seamed along the knuckles, the ice gone milky where it had melted and set again, the hairs on the back of it, the thumbnail.

And in the middle of it, where the fingers came together, a shape.

Not blur. She knew blur. Blur has softness in it and it has a direction. This had an edge, and the edge stayed an edge, and nothing inside the edge ever turned into a thing.

He tried it at three lengths. Wide, with the whole sheet in it and the hand small, and the shape was a shape. Midway. Then all the way out with the camera braced on the top of the boards and his elbow locked, and the hand came up huge and perfectly clear, and the shape sat in the middle of it at the same size it had always been.

"It's the glass," Hank said. "It's fogged inside the front element. Forty degrees in ten seconds, of course it's fogged—"

"Okay."

"It's the glass."

"Okay, Hank."

He wiped the front element with the tail of his shirt and shot it a fourth time and did not say anything about the fourth one.

---

Stacy came up to the boards with her arms folded and her bag on her forearm and stood there taking a reading off the room, which was her whole trade.

"They're not talking," she said.

"No."

"Curling's chatty. Curling is *shouting*. That's the entire sport." She put a hand up on the plexiglass and took it off again and looked at her fingertips. "Right."

Then she leaned over the boards and called across the ice.

"Hi! Yoo-hoo!"

Every one of them answered.

Both teams, the two coaches, the skip in the rings, all of them at once, without stopping what they were doing, without any of them looking up.

"Hey."

Ten voices, flat and pleasant, one syllable, and it came off the plexiglass and went round the tiers and out of the building, and then they went back to the game.

Stacy did not move for a second. "Okay," she said.

"Do it again," Hank said.

"I'm not doing it again."

She leaned over the boards instead and called, "Can somebody talk for a second? We're looking for our friend. Blonde, glasses, camera about this big."

One of the white team came up out of a crouch with the broom across his thighs. The visor came round.

"A person with a camera?"

"Yes."

"Yes." He shifted the broom. "Went through fast. Didn't stop."

"Where."

"He wanted the Screening Room." He said it the way a man tells you which bus. "Heading for the Records level. I think that's where the screens are."

Then he bent back down, and the game went on around him, and that was the end of it.

Behind her Hank said, "That's it. That's the room."

"That's the room," Burt said.

Alex had a postcard in her coat pocket and a hooded man's sentence in her head and now a third person in a third place putting the same words in the same order. Screens. A room of them. Records was a name, and a name was more than they had walked in here with.

"Write that down," she said, and realized she was talking to herself.

---

Hank went along the boards to the nearest coach, who was standing with a clipboard against his chest and his visor pointed at the ice.

"Hey. The hand." Hank waited. "What's with the hand?"

The coach did not look up.

"It's what happens when you lose."

He wrote something on the clipboard. The sweepers went past him bent at the waist and he watched them go and made another mark, and Hank stood there a while longer and got nothing else, because there was nothing else coming.

Alex did the arithmetic.

HOME 0. AWAY 0. Nobody had won anything. Nobody had lost anything. Whatever had been going on out there since before any of them came through that door had not once put a bulb behind either window, and the number over the far doors was not a time — no rink runs to five digits, and a rink clock counts down, and that one had been sitting at the top of itself since they came in.

Nobody here had lost. And the hand had come from somewhere.

She left it where it was. That was a thing she had started doing in this building, and she did not like the ease with which she now did it.

---

"So," Burt said. "How do we get across."

"We don't," Stacy said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I've heard the shape of it." She had her weight on one hip and the bag up on her arm like a shield. "One. These are not ice shoes. These are not any shoes for out there, and I'm not doing it in socks either. Two. I'm carrying everything that still works — the iron, the deck, all of it. Three." She held up three fingers and did not lower them. "A book, and then a car. Two for two. Every single time one of us has touched something in this mall, that thing has had us for lunch. And you want to go and pick up the frozen hand."

"Nobody's picking it up with a hand," Burt said.

"With *what*, then."

Ideas came, the way they do. Hank wanted the rope: tie four of them off at the boards, send one out long, and crack-the-whip him back if the ice took him — which he demonstrated with his arm, and which came apart in his own face by the end of the demonstration. Burt wanted a human chain, and had the plan mostly built before he arrived at the part where the last man in a chain is a man on his own. Hank's second idea was that they go on their bellies, all of them, like penguins, which is a real technique and he had seen it work, and Alex said where, and he said on television.

Djan said, "The umbrella opens."

"And?"

"You hold it in front of you." He had it half out of the bag already, the reflector, silver, the ribs folded down. "It's a wall. They'd have to come round it."

"They're playing a game, Djan. They're not coming."

"Then it costs nothing to hold it."

Alex tried to do the thing she was for.

Cost against odds. Who was quickest, who was steadiest, who was carrying what, what came off worst if the ice went out from under someone, and what the ten of them out there had actually done so far, which was answer hello in one voice and sweep. She could not get it to come out. Every version of it came out with the same term in it and the term was a person. The rope did not change that. The umbrella did not change it. The bellies did not change it. Somebody had to be the one out there, and the arithmetic did not care what was tied to them while they were.

She had three rules now.

Down is an escalator and up is a staircase, and the numbers count up as you go down. Nothing that belongs to the building gets touched with skin. And whatever you carry in here gets turned into an older version of itself, which is the building's way of telling you it has been through your things.

She had been keeping them in order. Not written down — in order, first, second, third, with room after the third.

You keep a list because you expect to add to it. Adding to it meant later. Later meant tomorrow, and *tomorrow* was a word with nothing to fasten itself to in a place where the light never went off, and she had been using it anyway, privately, in the back of her own head, for a while now. She stood on the wet rubber matting with her breath in front of her face and understood that some part of her had already agreed to be here a while and had not thought to run it past the rest of her.

"So the question," she said, "is who—"

Hank was already on the ice.

---

He had not said anything. He had taken the Neverfull off Stacy's arm on his way past, and Stacy had let him, and then had said, "Don't touch it. Use the bag. That's a real bag, Hank," and he said, "Understood," and stepped over the boards.

Burt turned to the ice and put his voice through it.

"All right, chuckleheads, break it up, get back to work — Hank, get out there and get that dag-on hand."

Nobody out there broke anything up. The sweepers swept.

Hank stopped four feet in and got his fist into his jacket, and Alex saw his knuckles go tight around the thing he kept in there, twice, the way another man crosses himself. Then he went.

His right boot went out from under him almost at once. She watched the whole of it from the boards with her taped palm flat on the top rail — the leg going, the arms coming up and out, the bag swinging round on its strap and pulling him further over than he already was, the camera swinging the other way and correcting it by accident. He did not fall. He came down onto the outside of the other boot, and got low, and stayed low, and found something that worked: short shoves, no lift, both arms out, all his weight underneath him. It was not walking. It got him down the sheet.

The teams did not look at him.

He went past the near rings and past the hog line and out into the middle of the ice, where a man in a green bib stepped round him at a distance of two feet, going somewhere else, and Hank shortened up and kept going and did not turn his head to watch him go.

At the far end he came in on it slow and dropped to one knee.

He had the mouth of the bag open with his forearm before the knee was down. Then one motion — the bag brought round low, flat to the ice, the leather lip of it going in under the frost, and the whole hand went into the bag the way you take up a dustpan's worth of glass, and the strap came up onto his shoulder, and he was standing.

There was nothing on the ice where it had been. A wet mark, going.

He turned and started back.

Both teams stood bolt upright.

All of them at once, mid-stride, mid-sweep, brooms hanging, the two coaches at the boards coming up off their clipboards in the same instant, and every opaque visor in the building turned and stayed turned, and not one of them moved again.
