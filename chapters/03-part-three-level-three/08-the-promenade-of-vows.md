## 08 · The Promenade of Vows
### *The Lovers*

> *CONGRATULATIONS! Ask any associate about financing. This vehicle has been prepared for its new owner. The keys have been left in the ignition as a gesture of trust.*

Hank had the camera up at the car before anybody had said a word about the car.

It was the best shot in the building and he had known it from the bottom of the escalator, walking toward it with the eyepiece already coming across his face. A white convertible on a lit platform, turning. He took it wide first, the platform's rim cutting the bottom of the frame, the spotlight coming down out of a ceiling with no lamp in it. Then he went in tight on the grille and let the car carry itself out of the shot on its own, which is a thing you cannot fake and cannot do twice.

What he had not got was the escalator.

He had been holding the rope. You cannot hold sixty feet of rope and work a Betacam, and the tape from the top of that escalator was twenty-two seconds of handrail and then his own knees and then the ceiling. The one time the mall did something to a machine while five people watched, and he had it on his shoulder, running, pointed at the floor. He had carried that down the last of the steps and he was carrying it still.

So he shot the car. The car was a gift. The car had the light on it already.

"It's an eighty-two," he said.

"It's a car, Hank."

"It's a LeBaron. Look at the hood." Nobody looked at the hood. It was flat and came to nothing at the front, no rise on it at all, and that was the year. He had grown up in a driveway with the back half of a car in it. That was not something he brought up.

Nobody had found the way down yet. Three storefronts, two corridors, white ribbon on every wall in streamers and in bows, and no escalator anywhere anyone could see. The air came sweet off the store to the left and sat in the back of the throat like syrup, and under the ventilation the piano did the little climb and the little fall, louder on this floor than the last one, and the fall came down further than he remembered it coming down. The car was the only thing in the plaza not fixed to a wall or a floor, so within a minute all five of them were standing around it, which Hank supposed was the point, if a thing like this had a point.

Alex went along the driver's side with her hands behind her back, the right one still wrapped in Burt's tape, grey and going furred at the edge.

"Keys are in it," she said.

"In it in it?"

"In the ignition. Hanging there." She leaned over the door without touching the door. "So. Showroom car. No salesman. No price on it. Keys sitting in it." She straightened and looked at the platform. "There's a lip on that thing. A foot deep. You could put anything under there."

"Under the car," Burt said, with interest.

"Under the platform. If you were hiding something in this room you'd hide it here." Which was three-quarters of a good reason for what she did next, and she said all of it out loud, so nobody could say afterward that she hadn't.

She reached over the door with the taped hand and turned the key.

The engine came up smooth. Warm, immediately, no cough in it. Hank had the camera on the platform edge and was swinging up to get her face when the second thing happened, and the second thing was that Alex began to drive.

Her arms came up. Both hands went onto the wheel and pulled hard left, and her whole body went with the pull, weight off one foot, shoulder dropping, chin coming round the way it does when a person is looking at where they are going. She screamed. Not a word; the noise you make with your mouth shut. She hauled back the other way and stamped down on nothing and screamed again and there was a sentence in it that time — *it won't* — and then she was hauling again, arms crossing, correcting, correcting.

The car idled and turned at the speed the platform turned, and the tires did not move on the surface of it, and the front wheels stayed where they had been, because Alex was standing outside the car and not holding a wheel at all.

Hank filmed it.

He was aware of doing it. He got her in three-quarter profile with the spotlight behind her, and stepped back one pace to keep her whole and let the platform bring her round to him, which was the shot. Stacy was behind his left shoulder saying *Alex. Alex. Honey.* Djan hadn't moved. Hank kept the frame level and the focus soft on the ribbon behind her and did not put it down, and if there was a word for how that felt he did not go looking for it, because the thing was happening and it was happening in front of a lens, and those two facts had never once come apart for him in his life.

"It's the book," Stacy said. "It's the book again, that's the book, that's—"

Burt handed her the paper sack.

Then he went. He didn't announce it. He put his head down and dropped his shoulder and crossed the carpet at a run with the backpack still on, and Hank swung to take him and got him dead center, the whole charge in frame from the fourth step.

He hit the car.

Nobody had accounted for the platform. Alex was eighteen inches left of where she'd been when Burt started, and the car had come round with her, and Burt went past her hip and into the door panel side-on with a sound like a dropped filing cabinet, and went up, and over, and into the back seat. His boots went last. The food sack came out of his hand and skidded under the platform lip, which was the only thing all day that went where Alex had said something might be.

For a second he was a big man folded into the footwell of a stationary car.

Then he sat up, slowly, both hands finding the top of the seat in front of him, and looked out over Alex's shoulder at the middle distance, and his face went pale in a hurry.

"Oh God," Burt said. "What have I done?"

Alex ran over something. Hank knew because she said so. She had gone past screaming into a running account of it, benches and a trash can and *the pillar, the pillar, hang on,* and behind her Burt had both hands braced on the seat back and was rising and falling slightly on the springs of a car that was not going anywhere, saying *okay*, saying *okay, honey*, saying *you're doing great*, in the voice of a man being driven at speed by somebody he loves and does not wish to insult.

Two of five. That was the arithmetic and it took Hank no time at all.

Hank put the camera down.

He set it on the carpet on its side, the way you never set a Betacam down, and did not turn it off and did not turn it round to face anything and did not think about it again.

Then he took the Leatherman off the loop of Burt's backpack, while Burt sat in the back seat telling Alex to watch the kid on the bike, and went round to the front and put his hand under the leading edge of the hood and found the catch on the second try, because he knew where the catch was on an eighty-two.

The hood came up.

And the car was moving.

It had not been moving when he walked up to it. He had walked up to a warm idling car on a turntable, and put his hand on the metal and lifted, and now the front end was swinging away under his palm at a speed he could not have put a number on, and the plaza was going by sideways, ribbon and glass and a bench coming up, and the fender he was holding was hauling him with it, and his feet knew they were on carpet and his stomach did not believe them.

"HANK." Stacy, off to the right, both hands cupped, the voice she used for the top of a piece. "HANK. Look at me. We've seen this twice now, you *know* it isn't real, it isn't real, it's not—"

He heard every word of it and it did not work.

It wasn't that he disagreed. He stood there with one hand locked on the fender and the sentence *nothing is happening* fully assembled in his head, correct in every particular, and it did not reach the parts of him that were holding on. He half-believed the engine instead. His hands went unsure — not shaking, worse than shaking, slow, the way hands go when you're working over a drop. And when he looked down into the engine bay, every single thing in it swam except one.

One thing stayed still: a black cap the size of a shot glass, low on the left, with a wire coming out of the top of it. He could not have told you its name and had never needed to, and it was the only object in that compartment with an edge he could trust.

He could have that, or he could step back and have nothing.

His thumb found the talisman in his pocket and squeezed it, which he had done in worse places than this and which had never once been for the talisman.

He committed. He got the blade out of Burt's multitool with his teeth, because his other hand was not letting go of the fender, and brought it down two-handed, and stabbed it.

The engine stopped.

It went out all at once, the way a light does, and the plaza came back and stood still. The platform turned. The spotlight came down out of nothing. Over their heads the piano got to the bottom of the little fall and started again from the top.

In the car, Alex let go of a wheel and looked at her hands.

Burt was still braced on the seat back. He looked at the upholstery under his hands, and at the trash can twenty feet away standing upright with nothing wrong with it, and at the arcs worn pale in the carpet all the way round the platform's rim, two of them, the way carpet goes when a thing has been turning on it a long time.

"We're in a mall," he said.

"We're in a mall," Alex said.

"Was I driving?"

"No."

"Was anybody driving?"

Nobody answered that. Burt got himself out over the door on the second attempt, and Alex stepped back from the driver's side and did not look at the key still hanging in the ignition, and Hank fished the food sack out from under the platform lip and gave it back to him.

Then Alex said, "Glove box."

It was locked. She worked the ring out of the ignition with the taped hand, which took her some doing, and the small key turned like it had been cut for it, because it had been.

The lid dropped open.

There was a cat in the glove box. Grey, lying on its side with its legs folded, filling the space the way a thing fills a space it has been fitted into, and dead. Not old-dead. There was nothing wrong with it at all. Its eyes were shut and its fur was clean and it looked like a cat asleep in a warm place, and it was not breathing, and it had been shut in there behind a lock that took a key off the ignition ring of a car nobody was selling.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Oh God," Burt said. "I killed it."

"You didn't."

"I opened the car."

"Alex opened the car."

"I got in the car," Burt said, and put his hand up over his mouth, and left it there.

Djan came round the hood and looked in past Alex's shoulder for longer than any of them, and then said, without any weight on it at all, "It doesn't mean anything. This place hands you a thing and it doesn't mean anything, and we can decide to make it mean something, or we can not."

Nobody took it up. Stacy had her hand at her collarbone. Alex looked at the cat with the expression she used on a document. Hank stood where he was with the camera on the floor eight feet away, and understood that a person could go a long way in this building believing things were being done *at* them, and be wrong the entire time.

Burt reached past Alex and closed the glove box, carefully, with two fingers, the way you close a door on a room where somebody is sleeping.

The piano went round. There was a bow the size of a wheel over the doors of the Ice Rink, and every bit of the ribbon in that plaza was white, and none of it had a date on it.

"There's a trunk," Stacy said.

She went round the back and put her hand on it and said, "I have never once had bad luck at a thing like this," and lifted the lid.

It was a hard hat. Yellow, scuffed on the crown, sweatband gone dark at the front. Nothing else in there. No spare, no jack, no mat, nothing under the mat, because there was no mat.

Burt picked it up in both hands and turned it over and looked inside it, and then put it on, and Hank watched it happen from four feet away.

It was not dramatic. That was what got him. Burt's shoulders came back and his chin came down, and he set the brim with one knuckle, and then he looked around the Promenade of Vows — the ceiling, the corridor mouths, the platform, the four of them — and Hank could see the room being converted while he stood in it. Not a plaza. A site. Phases, hazards, a schedule, and a man who had been left in charge of it.

"Who parked this car here?" Burt said.

Flat. All the pale had gone out of him.

"Hank. Get this piece of trash out of here."

"It's on a platform, Burt."

"Then get the platform out of here too, chucklehead. I got a walkway a foot off a moving object and no cone on it." He was already turning. "Stacy, you got heels on my floor, that's on me, I'm not writing you up. Djan. You're not standing where I can see you."

He did not stop. Hank got it inside a minute, and got it first, because Hank was the one being given the jobs. He was told to hold the tape end. He held it. He was told to walk the platform rim and see if it caught anywhere, and it didn't, and he had it done before he'd finished being told, and his hands were doing what he asked them to for the first time since the hood came up. Stacy had the bags redistributed and cinched in forty seconds. Alex was talking in her list voice, quick and clean, three things, in order. They were working like a crew — better than they had been in the Atrium on the first day with everything still in front of them, and nobody said one word about it, and Hank was as sure of it as he had ever been of anything and could not have told you what he was sure of.

"So here's the thing," Alex said. "The book grabbed Djan. The car grabbed you two." She counted them off against her taped fingers. "The hat's got Burt right now."

"Nothing's got me."

"You've given me four jobs."

"Because there's four jobs."

"You touch something in here," Alex said, "and it gets you. That's it. That's the rule. Everything in this building is a hand and we keep shaking it." She put the key ring on the platform edge and left it there. "Nobody touches anything on this floor."

Djan had gone by then. Hank saw him go and thought nothing of it, the way you stop thinking about a light that's always on. He came back while Alex was still talking, from the far corridor, hands in his jacket pockets, and stood at the edge of the group and said nothing at all.

Burt was looking down the near corridor at a run of storefronts with boards across the glass and a stack of something soft-cornered against a pillar. "Somebody quit in the middle of a job down there," he said, and turned away from it, and clapped once. "All right, team. Head on over to the ice rink. Gotta make sure that gate's wide enough for the Zamboni."

Nobody argued. Hank picked the camera up off the carpet on the way past and got it onto his shoulder, and they crossed the plaza toward the doors with the cold light behind the glass, in a loose diamond — Burt out front under the yellow brim calling the walk, Stacy on his shoulder, Djan wide, Hank a step behind with the bags balanced right for once. It was going better than anything had gone since the elevator. Somebody laughed at something Burt said about the ribbon.

Alex was last, and at the doors she turned her head and looked back at the car, still turning under its light with the key on the platform beside it, and then she came in with the rest of them.
