## 07 · Sixty Feet

> *MODEL 300-C. This unit travels between designated floors only. Length of travel: as required. In the event of unexpected lengthening, continue walking.*

"How's the hand," Stacy said to Djan, who was standing a few feet outside the group with his hands in his pockets.

That was the shape they had made at the top of the escalator without anybody deciding to: four of them in a loose arc facing the drop, Djan just off the end of it, not one pair of eyes on him.

"Fine," Djan said.

"Because that was a real chop. That was a chop out of a movie."

"I didn't go down."

"You went partway down," Stacy said, and there it was — Alex's shoulders coming off her ears about a quarter of an inch, and Burt looking up. That was all it took, most times. You say the embarrassing thing out loud in a silly voice and then everybody can breathe and get on with it.

"Anyway," she said. "I'm not going first."

---

She wanted to be clear that this was not a mood.

"One. These." She turned an ankle out. The heel was three inches and the tread of an escalator is a grid of ridges with slots between them, and she had come down the last one sideways like a woman getting off a boat. "Two. Whoever's first has nobody in front of them, which is the whole thing Burt said upstairs, so first isn't a punishment, it's a job, and it goes to whoever can run."

"I can run," Hank said.

"You cannot run backward with that on your shoulder."

"Three. There's a board back down that corridor with an arrow on it and a word under the arrow in red marker, and we agreed we weren't taking advice off a wall and then walked toward the arrow. If we're not taking it we could at least go slowly."

"That's not three reasons," Alex said. "That's one reason three times."

"It's a strong reason. It holds up."

The escalator went down out of the corridor mouth in a straight throat, tiled beige on both sides, and the light at the bottom was the same light as the light at the top, so there was nothing down there to descend toward. Way down there, on the wall at the base, a green sign with white letters. LEVEL THREE. She could read it from where she stood if she stopped talking and looked at it, so she stopped talking and looked at it, and then went back to talking.

Under the ventilation the piano did the climb and the fall and the small stop and started again, louder than in the concourse. It had also gone wrong in a way she could not have written down for money — the stop before the beginning was longer than it used to be, so that every thirty seconds there was a hole in it where she found herself waiting.

"We should shoot something first," Hank said.

"Here we go."

"No — genuinely. We've got walls, we've got the fountain, we've got twenty minutes of a bookshop that won't make sense to anybody who wasn't in it." He was already looking at the wall behind her the way he looked at things he was measuring. "Put Stacy in front of it. Two minutes. Where we are, how we got here, what's next."

"Yes," Stacy said.

"You don't have to—"

"Hank. Yes."

---

She got her compact out and did what could be done about it, which in that light was nothing, and did it anyway, because doing it was part of the machine that let her talk to a lens. Then she moved a step left so the escalator went down out of frame over her shoulder, because that was the shot and she wasn't going to make him ask.

"Sound," Hank said.

"There's no sound guy, Hank."

"Sound," Hank said, meaning himself, and put the camera up.

She let a breath go all the way out. Six years of car parks. There was a place she went half a second before, where her hands stopped being hers to think about.

"We came into this building through an elevator that isn't on the plan," she said. "It went down. It's still going down, in a sense. The numbers count up as you go under, which I would love somebody to explain to me." She let the corner of her mouth do a small thing. "We're looking for our intern. Jordan. Came in two weeks ago on a rumor and filed nothing since. Behind me is an escalator. There is no escalator going up, anywhere, on any floor, and I want that on the record."

"Keep going," Hank said, from behind it.

"We've been down here—" and she stopped.

It was not a stumble. She heard it happen from the outside the way she always did, the little air where the next word should have gone, and she went to fill it and there was nothing to fill it with. She did not know. Not roughly, not to the day. The light had not changed once since the doors opened, and nobody had asked, and she was not going to be the one, on camera, in front of the four of them.

"We've been down here a while," she said. "Back to you in the studio."

Hank held it a beat too long, then dropped the camera to his hip.

"That was good."

"It was okay."

There wasn't a studio. There was an office over a carpet showroom with eleven staff and a coffee machine somebody's mother had donated, and the words going away at the end had sounded exactly like an empty corridor, because that is what it was.

"Now can we go," Djan said.

"We skipped two stores," Alex said. "A record shop and a watch shop, lights on. Somebody in one of them knows what's on Level Four."

"Nobody in this mall has known what's on the next floor down. Not once."

"That's not an argument for going, that's an argument for asking better."

"Level Four is where he is."

"Level Four is where a lit button on a phone said he *was*."

Stacy watched all four of them get quieter instead of louder, which with this crew was worse, and she understood — the way she always understood these two minutes before anybody else did — that the disagreement was over and had been for a while, and that what was in the way was that nobody here wanted to be alone on a moving stair.

---

The rope came out of Djan's bag in a coil the size of a car tire, and he did not hand it over so much as put it down where Burt would find it.

"Sixty feet," he said.

"Sixty," Burt said, satisfied. "That's a working length." He ran a stretch of it through his hands, and Stacy got the sense he was checking it for something he was not going to find. Real rope. Theirs. Came in through the doors with them.

The plan took under a minute. Djan and Burt go down, tied on. Three hold at the top. Anything happens, they pull.

"Bowline," Burt said, and made one at Djan's waist without looking at his hands, and made another for the top end and gave it to Alex, who put her taped hand through it and closed her fingers. Hank got a wrap round his forearm. Stacy took the last of it in both hands with her weight back on her heels.

"Sixty feet gets you where," Alex said.

"Bottom's about forty steps. Sixty's generous."

"It's a good plan," Stacy said, warmly, because somebody had to say the plan was good, and because it was — it was the first thing all day that had a number in it.

Burt went down first. Djan went second, three steps back, one hand not quite on the rail.

---

It was fine for a long time. Stacy paid the rope out through her palms hand over hand, watching the two of them get smaller in the ordinary way that people get smaller, and Burt calling up the count of the steps because Burt would.

"Twenty."

"Twenty," Alex said back.

The coil at their feet went down and the rope kept going out, and Stacy looked past Burt's shoulders at the sign on the wall at the bottom, LEVEL THREE, white on green.

She could not read it.

She was reading a shape she remembered. The letters had gone to a smudge the size of a stamp, the way a sign does at the far end of a car park, and she had read that sign from this spot four minutes ago with a compact in her hand.

"Burt," Alex said.

"I know it."

"You're at the end of the rope."

"I know it."

Nothing lurched. That was the thing Stacy would keep, afterward, out of all of it. No jolt, no drop, no sound of a machine doing something it wasn't built for. The treads came up out of the plate at the speed treads come up. Burt and Djan were walking down a working escalator in a building with the lights on, and the bottom of it was where it had always been, and further away than it had been, and getting further away at about the pace of a person strolling.

The rope came off the floor. It came up out of the slack and went straight and then it went hard, and the loop bit into Alex's fingers inside the tape, and Hank said a word, and Djan turned around and up at them with an expression Stacy would have paid to photograph.

Her right heel skipped. Tick. Then it skipped again and did not come back, and the left one gave up the tile altogether, and she was going forward six inches at a time toward the comb plate with the strap of the Neverfull sliding down her shoulder.

"Don't let go," Burt shouted, from a distance that was wrong for how loud he was.

Nobody let go. That was the whole problem. Alex was braced with her boots crossways and her jaw set. Hank had a wrap of it and the camera in the other hand and was not going to drop either. Burt had Djan by the rope and the rope by both fists. Five adults, in a building, playing tug of war against a floor.

And nobody was deciding anything. Stacy could see the whole of it from where she stood, which was sliding: Alex would stay braced as long as the rope stayed hard, Burt would hold until it took his hands off, Hank was framing it, and Djan was watching the bottom go away with interest.

Her hand went into her bag.

She got the corner of the box under two fingers, the split seam of it, the cardboard gone furry from opening — five cards would take a minute, a minute on the floor, quiet, everybody shut up, and then she would know what to say and she would say it and they would move.

There wasn't a minute. There wasn't ten seconds. Her heel skipped a third time.

She got her hand out of the bag, took the rope in both fists, put her hip into it, and hauled.

Everything came at once. Alex came off the tile with a noise, Hank came with his arm still wrapped and the camera swinging into his ribs, and the three of them went over the comb plate in a heap of shins and rope and somebody's elbow in Stacy's back, and the treads took her weight and started carrying her down, and behind her nobody was standing on the landing at all.

"I wasn't rolling," Hank said, from somewhere near her feet. "I want it on record that I wasn't rolling."

She got herself up on two treads with a hand on each rail and spent the ride down going through the bag by feel — deck, makeup bag, iron, the whole inventory, twice — because the flap had been open when she went over and she was not going to be a person who lost things.

The escalator carried all five of them down together. Nobody split off.

---

Burt stepped off the bottom onto tile, and put a boot out, and pressed on the floor with it the way you test ice.

Then they all turned around and looked up it.

Thirty feet. A flight of stairs in a shopping mall, plate to rail, a beige wall on either side and a sign up there you could read from here. Djan pulled the rope in hand over hand and it kept coming, and he had a heap of it at his feet with the whole middle lying slack across four steps.

"Thirty," Burt said. "Thirty, near enough."

Nobody said the other thing.

---

The air arrived before the room did.

It was thick, and floral, and sweet past the point of sugar — the smell of a counter where they sell the candles and the little soaps, only there was a building's worth of it, warm, sitting in her throat. Stacy had worked eleven bridal fairs. She knew this smell professionally and it had never been this much of it.

The plaza was wide and the ceiling was low. Burt looked up at it and said nothing, which she noticed, and did not ask about.

The walls were hung with white ribbon. Not a swag or two by a doorway — all of them, every surface between the storefronts, streamers taped up in loops and bows wired to the trim at even spacing, the way a sale event gets decorated when someone cares a lot and then doesn't come back. Some loops had come down at one end and hung there. Nothing was dusty. Everything was faded on the side facing the room.

Three units with their lights on. Straight ahead, double doors with cold light through the glass and a rime on the inside — GREEN DAWN ICE. On the left, the shopfront putting out the sweetness, *Larme & Mucs*, tins stacked in the window in a pyramid. On the right, dresses, a wall of them, and behind the dresses a rail of tuxedos, and over the door in a script with a flourish, Forever White.

Two corridors let into two of the six sides.

No escalator. Not in either corridor mouth she could see into, not along any wall, not anywhere. She looked twice, then a third time, in a way she made look casual.

And nobody had said the thing about the stairs.

That was what she was chewing on while the other four spread out. Level Two's escalator had made her say *nope* out loud, to nothing, halfway down. This one had put sixty feet of rope on the ground and then set them all at the bottom together, politely, and not one of them had turned around and said *that was easier.*

She would rather have paid for it on the stairs. She could not have told anybody why, and was not going to try, and she put it where she put things, which was next to the sign she couldn't read.

In the middle of the floor a white convertible turned on a platform.

It was showroom-clean. There was a spotlight on it, hung on nothing, and nothing else in this room had a light of its own. The platform went round at the speed of a thing that had been going round a long time. Nobody stood beside it with a folder. Nobody was watching it. It came about until the hood was pointing at them, and kept going, and showed them the white side of it, and the back of it, and the other side, and then the hood again.
