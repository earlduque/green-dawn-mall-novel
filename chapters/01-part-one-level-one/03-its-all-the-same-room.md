## 03 · It's All the Same Room

> *HOW TO SEE IT: hold the image close to your face and let your eyes drift past the surface. Do not try. The picture is already there. Some viewers require several minutes. Some viewers never see it, and that is all right.*

Hank ran the wall back a fourth time and it was still a wall.

The viewfinder was a monochrome eyepiece the size of a matchbox, and everything that went onto the tape came back out of it grey. Grey pillar. Grey seam where two panels met and didn't quite. A grey pan, left to right, four seconds held on each section the way you hold it when you mean to use the shot. He had taken the Atrium's north wall from four positions and one crouch, and there was a wall on the tape.

It had been a different camera in the parking lot. Smaller, lighter, a thing you worked with one hand. It was a Sony Betacam BVW-300 now, with a shoulder pad worn shiny and a tape door that clacked, and Hank had said so twice on the way down and nobody had wanted it to be true, so he had stopped saying it. He knew the model. He could have given you the year.

"Anything," Alex said.

"It's a wall."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's a wall on tape."

Jordan had stood in this plaza three days ago and filmed the walls. They had that from Sophia and they had it off a screen in a room full of screens, fifteen minutes late and three days old: Jordan on the escalator, stopping, turning, lifting an old camera at nothing. So there was something on the wall. That was the whole of Hank's reasoning and he knew what it weighed.

He shot the wall again from the fountain side, with the light behind him. The tape was the one part of this he trusted and there was nothing in it.

Djan had the remote. It was a wand of moulded plastic, Sophia's parting gift, and the only thing anybody had worked out was that the top button changed its colour. Djan cycled it — orange, white, a red that came out pink under the Atrium's light — and stopped on green, and swept it along the wall at the pace of a man reading a shelf.

"Well?" Burt said.

"It's green," Djan said, and put it in his jacket.

Behind them the fountain ran with its basin already full to the lip, water going over the side in a sheet, nothing in the bottom of it but tokens packed edge to edge like fish scales. The light came down out of a ceiling with no lamp in it. Under the ventilation the piano went round again, the little climb and the little fall, louder here than it had been in the lift.

"Okay," Alex said. "We've done the thing. Now we're doing the thing where we keep doing the thing."

"One more angle."

"Hank."

"The low one. From the floor."

"You did the floor."

He had done the floor. He got the camera off his shoulder and held it by the handle, which is the worst way to hold a Betacam, and looked down the corridor of storefronts instead — dark glass, a photo studio, a place selling food nobody was eating, and past that a unit with racks in the window and a name over the door in cut vinyl.

---

Stacy came back from the escalator with her arms folded and her findings in order.

"So there's a sign at the top and a sign at the bottom," she said. "Up here says Level One. Down there says Level Two."

Burt turned that over. "Two's under one."

"Two's under one."

"That's backwards."

"It's only backwards if you look at it," Stacy said. "And here's the thing." She held up a hand, flat, and tipped it. "It's not two escalators. It's one, going down. Up is a staircase, way over the other side, past the fountain. I walked it. It's a staircase."

Hank took the escalator from where he stood, the long throat of it going down into light the same colour as the light up here, and it made an ordinary shot. Handrail, treads, the brush strip along the edge. Whatever was wrong with it was not going to be wrong on tape.

"There's an elevator too," Stacy said. "Mall one. Signposted and everything."

"Three ways out of a floor," Burt said, with approval, and hitched the pack. "That's better than most places."

"Stores first," Alex said. "Jordan was in here. Jordan was in something."

---

The door of Magic Eye had a chime on it that went *ding-ding*, two notes, the second one flat.

It was racks. Floor to ceiling on three walls, freestanding racks down the middle at chest height, every one of them full of posters in that mottled repeating pattern Hank remembered from dentists' offices — the same slick paper, the same laminated card taped up at the end of each rack telling you how to see it.

They were not dolphins. Alex had one out of the rack within about four seconds and turned it toward the ceiling and said, flatly, "That's the Mona Lisa."

Hank looked at it. It was pattern, a field of shapes repeating with a lean in them, and if there was a woman in there she was staying in there.

"Starry Night," Stacy said, going past, not even slowing down. "Oh, that's nice actually."

Burt stopped in front of one on the back wall with his hands behind him like a man at a memorial, looked at it a while, said nothing at all, and moved on. Hank went and stood where Burt had stood. American Gothic, said the sticker in the corner. He got the pitchfork eventually and then lost it again.

He had never been able to do it. Not once, not at nine, not in the dentist's, not now. He could recite the instructions; he had held things at arm's length and up against his nose until his eyes watered, and what he got was paper. So he did what he did, which was put the camera on it, wide and tight, because the tape did not need to see the picture for the poster to be a thing that had been there.

The chime went *ding-ding* behind him, flat second note, and it was Stacy's phone.

She stopped where she was, three feet inside the door. "Guys."

"What."

"I have a bar."

Five people converged on one square foot of carpet, and four of them pulled out phones, and four phones had nothing. Hers had one bar. She held it over her head, then out to the side, then down at her hip, and the bar stayed, and she moved her left foot eight inches and the bar went, and moved it back and the bar came.

"Nope," she said. "Nope. I live here now."

She dialled. Hank watched her face do the thing it did on camera when she was about to be pleased, and then not do it. She held the phone out so they could hear, and out of it came a hiss, and under the hiss a room — a wide room, air moving, a piano doing the little climb and the little fall — and three seconds of that, and then nothing.

"Who'd you call?" Burt said.

"The office."

She tried twice more and got the same room both times. Then she typed, and sent, and the message went with a small confident noise and sat there being sent to nobody, and she typed another and sent that too, and started down through the apps one at a time with her thumb, and did not move her feet again for the rest of the time they were in that store.

Hank got a shot of her doing it. She was good in it, the way she was always good in it, phone up, chin up, one heel planted like a survey stake in a shop full of paper.

"Hank." She had gone still. "My texts are from next week."

"Say that again."

"My old ones. The timestamps are all in the future." She scrolled. "They're not even the same wrong. This one's four days out. This one's a month. It's just wrong. The messages are the messages. I remember every one of them."

Alex looked at it a long moment and handed it back and said, "Don't clear anything," which was not a plan, and everybody knew it, and was better than nothing to say.

Burt got the paper sack out and offered it round and Stacy took something without looking and held it in her free hand and did not eat it.

---

There was a counter at the back and a man behind the counter, and he looked apologetic before anybody had asked him for anything.

Hank went up because that was the job. Middle-aged, thin, a polo shirt in the shop's green, a name tag turned around backwards on its clip. He said hello. Then his eyes came up.

The irises shifted. That was the first thing, and it was not a thing Hank could put anywhere. They moved inside the eye like something floating on a liquid, and there were rings in them, more rings than there should be, bands of colour going in toward the middle — and in the left eye the pupil was not at the middle. It sat out at the edge of the third ring, and the middle was the same green as the rest. When the man turned his head to nod at the racks, the eyes went half a beat late, and then caught up.

Hank had the camera on his shoulder and did not raise it.

"Dude," he said. "What's wrong with your eyes?"

"What's wrong with yours?"

"Nothing's wrong with mine."

"Well." The man smiled, and it was an apology. "You spend enough time in here, that's kind of what happens. Sorry. It puts people off."

Hank got the camera up then, because that was the one thing in this store worth having, and the man was patient about it and did not look into the lens.

"Blonde kid," Hank said. "Glasses. Camera like this but older. Would've been in here."

"Oh, sure." No pause. None. "Stood right where you are for a long time. Went all the way round the racks twice, then all the way round again the other way." The eyes drifted at the wall and lagged coming back. "Kept saying the same thing while he did it. Under his breath. Kind of upset."

"Saying what."

"It's all the same room."

Alex had come up on Hank's left and stopped.

"That's it?"

"That's it. Over and over. Then he went out and down the escalator." He said it the way you'd tell somebody where the restrooms were. "That was three days ago."

"Down there," Hank said. "Level Two."

"Down, anyway."

Hank kept the camera running. He had it — the exact words in the exact voice, on tape, and he knew that when he played it back it would be a man in a green polo shirt saying six words, and no part of the tape would carry what the man's eyes were doing.

He asked the price of the posters, mostly to keep the man talking, and the man said he'd have to check, and did not check.

---

Alex stopped moving.

She was two feet to Hank's right and she had stopped mid-sentence, and her face had gone loose in a way he had only seen on people asleep on trains. Her eyes were open and pointed past the clerk's head at the wall behind him. Not at his eyes. Past him, at a patch of wall.

The clerk went on being apologetic about poster prices.

Hank turned his head to look at her, which is the position a camera operator spends his whole life avoiding. Her mouth was slightly open. She was breathing. Then she blinked once, hard, made a small sound in her throat, and walked directly at the wall.

She went into the corner where a rack stood bolted flat between two others, put both hands into the middle of the posters, pushed them apart like curtains, and reached in behind them up to the elbow.

"Alex," Burt said.

"Hang on."

"You all right?"

"Hang on."

Hank came round and framed her from behind, arms in the rack, and did not know what he was shooting, and shot it anyway. When she came out she had one thing between two fingers, held away from her body, and she said, "Somebody put this here."

---

It was a postcard.

The front was one of the posters, shrunk to fit — the mottled field, the lean in the pattern, Starry Night by the sticker on the corner, which Hank took her word for. The card had been in there long enough for the top edge to go furred where it had rubbed against the poster in front of it.

The back had writing on it. Hank got in close over Alex's shoulder, close enough to read it in her hand, and did the thing he did with a shot he was going to have to use, which was take the whole of it in as a picture before he took it in as words.

Print, not cursive. Small capitals, cramped, the whole of it leaning backward — every downstroke tilted left, as if the hand had been pulling away from the words while it made them. Every letter T was crossed above the letter instead of through it, a bar floating over the top, and there were nine of them and all nine were the same. The lines ran out of room at the right edge and the letters shrank to fit, so the last two or three words of every line came out squeezed and half the height of the first ones, and the final line dropped and crowded into the margin at the bottom. Ballpoint, blue, pressed hard enough that Hank could feel the words from the picture side with his thumb, four ridges of them, like braille.

It said:

*Down, past the escalator, the room where everything is a screen. They're there. Or they were. I couldn't follow.*

Nobody said anything for a while. Over their heads the piano finished the little fall and started again at the top.

"That's not the clerk's," Alex said. "He's got a pad on the counter. It's not his."

Djan put his hand out for it and Alex gave it over, and he held it near his chest and tipped it to the light, and went on holding it. Djan looked at things longer than the things were worth. Hank had learned to leave it alone.

"It isn't Jordan's," Djan said.

"You're sure."

"His J's have a hook. On the tea cups." Djan turned the card over and looked at the front of it, which had nothing on it to look at. "This is somebody else."

Hank worked it through twice, out of order, the way you cut a sequence in your head: a person had written this; the person had come into this store; the person had known enough to write *the room where everything is a screen*, which was more than the crew knew standing here; the person had put the card behind a rack where you would only find it if you knew to look; and then the person had not gone down.

Somebody had been all the way here, ahead of them, on their own.

"Since when," Burt said. He had taken the hard fact and gone straight past it to the part that was his. "Before Jordan or after?"

Nobody had that. Stacy said, from her square foot, without looking up, "Ask the guy," and Hank asked him, and the man tilted his head — the eyes going late, catching up — and said that people came in, and that he wished he could be more help. Which was true. He was sorry. It was the whole of his face.

---

The clerk smiled at Alex as she came past the counter, and said, without being asked anything, "That's that street-smart side of you, isn't it, Alex."

She stopped in the doorway with her hand on the glass and did not turn around, and then she went out under the flat second note of the chime. Nobody had told him her name. Hank had the camera down at his hip and the tape was still running, and it got the words and it got the chime, and it did not get what happened to the back of Alex's neck.

Across the corridor the food place had its shutter half up, a uniform on a hook inside with the hat still on the shoulder, and the air coming out from under the shutter was old fryer oil, cold. Nobody went in.

They stopped by the fountain, because none of them was ready to be at the top of the escalator yet.

"Film the card," Alex said, holding it out. "Both sides. In case we lose it."

Hank looked at it in her hand. The furred edge. The nine floating bars over the nine letter T's. The last line, dropped and crowded into the margin because the writer had run out of card and had gone on anyway, in a hand that was not Jordan's:

*I couldn't follow.*

He did not raise the camera.
