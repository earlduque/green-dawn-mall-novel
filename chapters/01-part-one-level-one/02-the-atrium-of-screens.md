## 02 · The Atrium of Screens

> *GREEN DAWN MALL WELCOMES YOU. Over four hundred stores and services. Tokens are available at all fountains. Please do not remove tokens from the property. Have a green day.*

The light was wrong, and Stacy noticed the light before she noticed the room, because the light was going to be her problem all day.

It came from everywhere. No fixtures, no windows, no warm patch on the floor to tell you where a bulb was hiding. It would put a shadow under a person's eyes and keep it there as long as you cared to stand still. She turned her face a few degrees to find the angle that worked, and there wasn't one, and she filed that under things to solve later, which was where most of her life went.

Her heel came down and slid an eighth of an inch.

Water. A film of it across the tile, spreading toward them in a shape with fingers, and she moved two steps sideways onto dry floor in a way that read as a decision. Six years of standing in front of a camera in car parks. You learn to do that.

She looked up, because the room made you.

The ceiling was not there. There was one, presumably, but the walls went up past a second level and a third and kept going, storefront over storefront, rail over rail, until the whole business went pale and quit being visible. She got to four tiers of railing, lost her count, and did not start again.

"So," she said, to hear a voice in it. "It's a mall."

"It's a mall," Hank agreed, from behind the camera.

"Somebody works here," Alex said. She was turning on the spot with her hands in her coat pockets. "We find whoever works here. We ask about Jordan. We're home for dinner."

Under the ventilation — and the ventilation was loud, a long exhale coming down out of all that height — the piano was still going. Up a little, down a little, the small stop, and then the beginning again. In the lift she had needed to hold still to hear it. Out here it was simply on, the way a radio is on in a shop, at the volume of a thing nobody has thought about in years. Thirty seconds, give or take. The same thirty seconds.

She had started calling it the Jingle somewhere between the doors opening and now, which she noticed, and did not examine.

And there were screens. On the pillars, in the shopfronts, hung in banks off the railings of every level she could make out, one of them showing a corridor with the light in it exactly as flat as the light she was standing in. It smelled of plastic — not of anything in particular being plastic, just of the material, warm, all through the air.

---

The water was the fountain's fault.

It sat in the middle of the floor, a wide basin, three tiers, with a low wall around it so children could be told not to climb on it. The top tier had a crack across it, and the water was coming up out of the crack instead of the spout and going over the lip on all sides at once, without pressure, the way a bath goes over when somebody has walked out of the room.

Under the water the whole basin was gold.

Stacy crouched, one hand flat on the wall, one hand keeping the strap of the Neverfull up on her shoulder. Not coins. Tokens — stamped brass a shade bigger than a quarter, a leaf on one side, GREEN DAWN on the other, and *1 TOKEN* under that. The floor of the basin was paved with them, three deep, and more out on the wet tile where the overflow had carried them.

"Arcade money," Hank said, filming it.

Burt walked the perimeter looking at the ground. "Where's it going," he said.

"Where's what going."

"The water. It's coming off a pipe, and a pipe's got a valve on it, and it's been running long enough to get under the door of a shop." He said it in the tone of a man reading a work order. "Somebody ought to see to that."

Djan photographed the fountain, then the wet floor, then picked a token out of the water and put it in his shirt pocket without a word. Alex looked at the basin with her hands in her pockets and left them there. Burt lifted one out, held it in his palm a moment, and dropped it back in.

"Not wishing?" Stacy said.

"Got what I need."

She picked one up herself. Dried it on the hem of her jacket. Held it, and threw it in, and it went in without a sound because there was already too much water for a sound.

"You know that's not a real fountain," Alex said.

"It's got water in it."

She had closed her eyes to do it. Nobody had seen that, she was fairly sure, and what she had wished for was not going home. It had come up out of her before she could put something more respectable in its place, and nobody asked, and she wouldn't have said.

---

The plaza had corners, and she counted them because there was nothing else to do while Hank finished with the fountain: six. Three of them opened into corridors. One of the three went back to a set of steel doors in a recess with a call button worn pale in the middle.

Hank came up with a map. There was a rack of them bolted to a bench, and the top one came out stiff the way paper does when nobody has taken one in a long time. He got it open and Stacy read it upside down over his arm.

*THE ATRIUM OF SCREENS*, it said, over a shape that was more or less the shape of the room they were in. *You are here.* A list of stores down one side, in type small enough to need better light than this.

On the map, the Atrium had four corridors.

"That's a misprint," Hank said.

"Sure."

Alex had gone off toward the corridor mouth on the left, and came back at a pace that was almost but not quite normal walking.

"There's an escalator," she said. "Down."

"Down to what?"

"To a mall. There's a mall down there. There's a plaza and shopfronts and a rail, and it's got the same light in it."

Nobody said anything about going down it. It sat there in the conversation like a dish nobody was reaching for.

---

They took the corridor with the shops in it, because Stacy pointed out that a person was likelier to be in a shop than in a fountain.

The corridor had benches down the middle of it and potted trees between them, and the trees were not plastic and not alive either. On the left: a photo studio, PORTRAITS WHILE YOU WAIT, the window hung with the families of nobody — a graduate holding a scroll, a couple in knitwear, a baby on a rug with its fist in its mouth, all of them lit better than anything had been lit since she came in. On the right: KEVIN'S GREASE, a menu board with prices in tokens, a fryer, six stools, and a smell off it that was thirty years of somebody else's lunch. A payphone with a card behind the plastic listing four digits for MALL SERVICES.

Everything was 1985. Not decorated like it — dated like it, in the fonts and the fittings and the brown, and she couldn't have proved it and would have bet the paper on it.

Fourth along, in gold letters across the glass: *Télévision Libertés.*

"That's a TV shop," Hank said, with feeling.

"Is it French?"

"It's got an accent on it."

The door dinged when they went in, a real two-note ding, and Stacy did the thing she does when she goes through a door, which is to be ready to be liked.

---

It was not a TV shop.

There was a counter, and behind it the room went back further than the shopfront had any right to, and every wall was screens. Small monitors, racked three high and further along than she wanted to count, some black-and-white, some colour, all on. A corridor. A corridor. A staircase with a bag of something on the third step. The inside of a store, empty of people. A plaza that was not this one.

And in the bottom right corner of each screen a set of numbers, burning in white, counting.

Stacy found the fountain on the fourth screen along. Good angle, high and to the side, the crack and the overflow and the water reaching across the tile, five people around the basin, and one of them crouched with a hand flat on the wall, keeping her bag up on her shoulder, closing her eyes over a token.

She watched herself do it. Her posture was worse than she'd thought and her hair was doing exactly what she'd expected in that light.

"Guys."

"I see it," Alex said. She was already up close to the numbers in the corner. "This one's fifteen minutes ago. That one's an hour. This one's — forty minutes. None of them are now. Not one of them is now."

Hank stood in the doorway and waved out at the corridor, then came back and watched the screen with the corridor on it, in which nobody was waving.

On the screen third from the left a woman stood in a corridor with her back to the camera, and she was standing in that same spot on the screen beside it, which was somewhere else.

"There's no camera in here," Djan said.

He was right. Nothing on any wall in the room showed the room, or the counter, or the five of them standing in front of it. Whatever this place was for, it was not for looking at itself.

---

"Oh — hi. Hi. Sorry, I'm right here, I'm just under here."

She came out from under a bench with a screwdriver in one hand and a bundle of cable over her wrist, and straightened up, and pushed the hat back off her forehead. A bowler hat, purple, felt, worn by a woman who had decided about it long ago and was not accepting notes.

"Sophia," she said. "You're not lost, are you? People come in here lost."

Her accent sat somewhere south of somewhere. Stacy had a good ear and could not put a pin in the map, and let it go, because the woman had a room of screens behind her and was, at a glance, delighted to have visitors.

"We're lost," Stacy said. "Comprehensively. Is this your shop?"

"It's not a shop." Sophia looked around at it as though checking. "It's not mine, either. I don't work here. I was assigned this task."

"By?"

"They didn't say." She said it lightly, the way you'd say a bus was late. "It's a lot of connections, is the thing. Half of them are corroded and the other half go somewhere I haven't found yet."

"New management?" Stacy said, warmly, with the smile she used for people who ran things badly and knew it.

Sophia laughed. It came half a beat late and stopped early, and her hand went up and turned the hat a quarter-turn and took it off and put it back on. "Sure," she said. "Something like that." Then she was busy with the cable, and the cable did not need her.

Stacy left it. That was the whole trick, and the only thing she had ever been genuinely good at: you leave it, you hand them something else to say, and they give you three more things later. She asked about the screens. Sophia lit up.

"Oh, I don't know anything about the archive. I'm not archive, I fix things." She rapped a monitor with two knuckles. "They're all behind. I can't get one of them to be now. I swapped two over and the delay stayed with the wall, not the screen, which — " she stopped. "You don't want to hear it."

"I do."

"You don't, but it's kind of you." She wiped her hands down her cardigan. "It's all out of date by the time I get to it. That's the other problem. I keep a dictionary and it goes off."

"A dictionary."

Sophia took a notebook out from under the bench, fat, rubber-banded, and let it fall open. Pages of words in a small hand, and under the words, other words, and lines through those. *ATRIUM OF SCREENS* had been written and struck out and written again with something else, and struck out again.

"It rearranges," she said. "Every time somebody new comes in, it does it again. Not much, most times. A corridor turns up, a store's on the other side. But you can't keep a list of a place that does that, so what I've got is a list of what it used to be." She looked at the page. "It was a good dictionary."

"Where are you from?" Stacy asked, because she liked her now and it was the next thing you asked.

"Further along," Sophia said. "It's not — later? Later than this. It's from the future, where I'm from."

"Okay," Stacy said, because there wasn't a version of not saying that which was less strange, and because it had been said in the same voice as the corroded connections.

Burt said, "Ma'am, we're looking for a kid. Blonde, glasses, camera he doesn't put down."

"Oh, yes." Sophia closed the notebook on her finger. "Three days back. Went down the escalator." She pointed the way Alex had come from, without looking, which meant she knew the room. "Stopping and starting. Filming the walls, which I did wonder about. And no, before you ask — not since. I'd have seen. Not much comes through here."

Nobody said anything for a second and the piano got to the small stop and started again from the beginning.

"It's plastic that worries me," Sophia said.

Stacy blinked. "Sorry?"

"For all this." She waved the screwdriver at the racks, the housings, the trim. "It isn't a great material. Everybody acts like it lasts. It doesn't last, it just doesn't rot, and those aren't the same thing. It breaks. You put any weight on it and it goes all at once, and then you've got a piece of it in your hand and nothing to do with it." She shook her head at the monitors. "I keep saying. Nobody's asking me."

"Right," Stacy said.

"Anyway." Sophia was reaching under the bench again. "You should have this. I found it in the housing of the second one along and I've tried it on every screen in the room."

It was a remote. Grey, ribbed, a row of coloured buttons, heavier than it looked. When she pressed it the top went green, then red, then a blue that was almost white, and nothing in the room did anything at all.

"It's not for these," she said. "I'd only lose it."

Hank took it before anyone could weigh in.

---

Djan had gone down the racks while all this was happening, and he was standing in front of one screen with his hands at his sides. "Alex."

"What."

"The numbers on this one."

They were the wrong length. Every other screen in the room was counting off numbers that meant a time today. This one had a date in front of it, and the date had three days on the wrong side of it.

The screen showed a rail, a landing, and the top of an escalator going down out of frame, shot from high and behind.

"That's the one I found," Alex said. "That's the escalator."

A figure came into the top of the picture with a coat on and a bag across the body, and stopped, and Stacy knew the shoulders before she had anything else — because Jordan carried a camera the way other people carry a cigarette, always up, always half-raised, never put down, not in the office, not at lunch. Glasses. Fair hair going white in that light.

Jordan stepped onto the escalator and went down two steps and turned and put the camera on the wall.

Held it there. Panned along it. Went down another few steps, and stopped again, and did the wall again.

Then the head went below the level of the rail and the picture had nobody in it.

Nobody in the room said anything. In the corner of the screen the numbers kept going, fifteen minutes behind an afternoon three days gone, and every other screen on that wall was showing a mall that had already finished happening, and there was not one thing anywhere in that room that showed them where they were standing.
