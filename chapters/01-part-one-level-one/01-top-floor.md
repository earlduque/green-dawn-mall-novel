## 01 · Top Floor

> *CERTIFICATE OF INSPECTION. This conveyance was last examined on a date the sticker no longer shows. Capacity 2,500 lbs. In an emergency, remain calm and press the button marked with a bell.*

There were five of them. Alex knew this because she had counted on the stairs, and again in the fourth-floor corridor, and once more now, and the counting was not something she had decided to start doing.

Burt was carrying everything, which was not a division of labor anyone had agreed to. It had simply turned out that if you set a bag down within six feet of Burt Calloway, the bag went onto Burt. He had the backpack, and Hank's spare battery case, and a paper sack of something he had cooked at home the night before and brought for everyone, which nobody had opened, and which he offered again at the top of every flight of stairs.

Hank was filming the stairs. There was no reason to film the stairs. Hank had explained the reason twice, at length, with the words *establishing* and *texture* in it, and Alex had stopped listening on the grounds that it was cheaper than arguing.

Djan was a corridor ahead. Djan was always a corridor ahead. He came back sometimes.

Stacy had her phone against her ear and was not talking to anyone. Alex had watched her dial. The number had four digits.

That was five, including herself, and the reason Alex kept counting was that two weeks ago the number had been six.

---

Jordan had been theirs for eleven months. Unpaid for the first four, which was the kind of thing the paper did and then apologized for at Christmas. Jordan brought tea round in the afternoons in cups they had labeled themselves in small cramped handwriting so nobody would take the wrong one, and asked questions in editorial meetings that made the room quiet, and did not put the camera down, ever, not in the office, not at lunch, not once.

The last thing Jordan filed was a note. Not a story. A note, four lines, about a rumor.

The last confirmed sighting was a badge swipe on the fourth floor of the annex, at nine minutes past eleven in the morning, two weeks ago.

Alex had been the one who signed off on the assignment. It had taken her about a minute and a half. She remembered it being a Tuesday and she remembered thinking it was a nothing errand, a quarter-page at best, and she had not said any part of that out loud to anyone since and did not plan to.

---

They had come in through the mall, because that was the only entrance that was still unlocked.

It was a real mall, or it had been. Two levels, a skylight furred with pollen, a directory board with half its little plastic letters gone. The sign out on the road had been sun-bleached past reading for years — you could see where the letters had been, the way you can see where a picture used to hang.

Most of the units were empty. The empty ones had brown paper taped over the glass, and the paper had gone the color of weak tea, and behind one sheet somebody had left a mannequin standing with one arm up.

The fountain was off. There was change in the bottom of it, under an inch of standing water, and Alex noticed that she looked at it, and noticed that Hank filmed it, and noticed that Stacy stopped walking for a second and then didn't say anything.

Out past the glass doors the parking lot went on for a long way in white lines and nothing else. Four cars. One of them was theirs.

"So what is it," Stacy said. "What's the piece. If we find him."

"When," Burt said.

"When. What's the piece."

Nobody answered for a while, because the honest answer was that there wasn't one yet, and because the paper had eleven staff and a circulation you could fit in this parking lot, and because every single one of them had at some point in the last two weeks caught themselves thinking about what a missing-intern story would do for the paper, and had been ashamed of it, and had gone on thinking about it.

"Local paper finds what the police didn't," Hank said. "That's the piece. That's a wire pickup."

"That's a wire pickup," Stacy agreed.

"That's if he's findable," Alex said, and heard how it came out, and did not take it back.

Djan had stopped in front of the brown-papered unit with the mannequin in it. He looked at it for longer than the thing warranted, then took one photograph, then came back.

"Something?" Alex said.

"No."

"Nice light," Hank said, meaning the skylight.

"It's a mall, Hank."

"It's got nice light."

The annex was attached at the north end, past a shuttered pretzel counter, through a set of doors with a bar across them that wasn't locked either. Somebody had been renovating the annex and had stopped. There was a sawhorse in the lobby with a length of pipe balanced across it, and a stack of ceiling tiles going soft at the corners, and a printed notice taped to the wall that said work would be completed and gave a date that had already happened.

Burt read the notice twice on the way past. He didn't say anything about it. Alex would think about that later.

---

The fourth floor was offices. Three suites, two of them vacant, one of them a leasing agency with the blinds down and a sign in the window giving a phone number. Alex had already called it. The phone number rang in a room somewhere and nobody had ever picked it up.

Jordan's badge had swiped at the stairwell door on this floor and never swiped anywhere again. That was the whole of it. That was the entire investigative basis for five adults standing in a beige corridor at half past two in the afternoon.

"Anything?" she said.

"Carpet's newer at that end." Djan had come back from wherever he had been. "By about ten years."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning they replaced it at that end."

There was a directory beside the stairwell, screwed to the wall, white letters slotted into black felt. FOUR — three lines, two of them blank where the plastic letters had been taken out. FIVE — and then nothing. Not blank the way the vacant suites were blank. Blank the way a line looks when the person filling in the board got to it and moved on.

The building was five floors. Alex had counted the windows from the parking lot, and then counted them again, because from the parking lot it had looked like six.

"There's a lift," Djan said.

---

It was at the end of the corridor, past where the carpet changed, in a recess that Alex would have walked past. Single door, brushed steel, a call button worn pale in the middle. Above it, an inspection certificate in a plastic sleeve, and the certificate had a date on it once.

There was no lift on the directory. There was no lift on the fire plan by the stairwell either, which Alex checked, and which had a little arrow diagram of the fourth floor with a stairwell and a corridor and no recess at the end of it at all.

Hank got the camera up.

"Okay," Alex said. "So we don't get in that."

"We don't?" Hank said.

"It's not on the plan."

"Right, but it's *here*."

"Those are two different facts and you're allowed to hold both."

Burt set the bags down, which took a while. "I'll say the thing nobody's saying," he said. "Kid's badge swiped on this floor. Kid didn't come out. That's a lift that ain't on the paper. That's not a coincidence, that's a lead."

"That's a lead," Hank agreed.

"That's a hole in the ground with a lead painted on it," Alex said.

Stacy had her hand in her bag. Alex saw her get the deck about halfway out — the corner of the box, the split seam where the cardboard had gone soft from being opened — and then push it back down under the makeup bag and take her hand out and fold her arms.

"Nope," Stacy said, to nobody. "Not doing that here. Not for a lift."

"You want to draw on a lift?"

"I want somebody else to decide," Stacy said, cheerfully, and that was the most honest sentence anyone had said all day.

Djan pressed the button.

Alex opened her mouth to object to that and found that the objection had gone somewhere. It was a strange thing to notice — the shape of a sentence still sitting there in her chest with the words taken out of it.

The doors opened.

---

She was inside.

That was the part. Not *she got in* — she was in, and so were the other four, and there had been no interval. There was the corridor with the pale button, and then there was the inside of a lift with five people in it, and between those two things Alex could not locate anything at all. Not a decision. Not a step. Not the sound of her own shoes changing from carpet to floor.

For about half a second nobody moved, and Alex understood with total clarity that all five of them were experiencing the same thing, and that all five of them were about to pretend otherwise.

Burt got there first. "Well, all right," he said, and hitched the backpack, as though he'd walked in.

"Tight in here," Stacy said, and checked her hair in the steel, as though she'd walked in.

Hank was already filming the panel, as though he'd walked in.

Djan stood with his hands at his sides and looked at Alex, and did not say anything, and did not pretend.

Alex looked away first.

---

The panel had more buttons than the building had floors.

There was no arrangement to it that Alex could make work. Twenty-odd buttons in four uneven rows, some of them numbered and some of them not, and the numbers did not run in order, and three of the buttons had no legend on them at all — just the little round dish of them, worn pale in the middle the same way the call button had been worn pale, which meant somebody had pressed them a great many times.

There was a button with a bell on it. There was a button with a bell on it, and a slot for a key, and a phone behind a small hinged door, and the phone had a cord, and the cord went into the wall.

"Don't press anything," Alex said.

"Wasn't going to."

"Hank."

"I *wasn't*."

The doors closed.

It smelled of plastic. Not of a plastic thing — not the carpet-and-warm-electrics smell of a working lift, which Alex knew, which everyone knows. It smelled the way a new appliance smells when you get the packaging off it, the sealed-bag smell, the smell of something that has never been used and has been waiting a long time to be. It was strong enough to taste.

"Anybody else—" Burt said, and stopped, and turned his head slightly, the way you do when you want to hear something without admitting you are trying to.

"Yeah," Djan said.

Under the ventilation there was music.

It was piano. It was cheerful and it was not very good and it was mixed so low that Alex could only get it in pieces, four or five notes at a time, and it did a small ascending figure and then a small descending one and then it stopped, and then it started again from the beginning.

She listened to it start again from the beginning twice, and got as far as timing it — about thirty seconds, she thought, maybe a little less — before she made herself stop.

"That's been playing the whole time," Stacy said.

"Since when?"

"Since the mall. Downstairs. By the fountain." Stacy was very still, which for Stacy was extraordinary. "It's the same one. It's just louder in here."

Nobody had pressed anything.

Alex was certain of it in the way she was certain of very few things, because she had been watching the panel and she had been watching all four pairs of hands, and that was her whole job, and nobody had pressed anything.

The floor went down.

It went down smoothly and for a long time. Long enough that Burt got the paper sack out and offered it around and Hank took a thing out of it and held it without eating it. Long enough that Stacy checked her phone twice. Long enough that Alex counted the five of them again, twice, and then made herself stop doing that too.

Djan was looking at the ceiling of the lift. There was a hatch up there, and a grille, and the light came out of the grille evenly and did not flicker.

"We're past the ground," he said.

"We're past the ground by a lot," Alex said.

Nobody said the next part. It was very quiet, apart from the piano, which had started again from the beginning.

Then the car settled, the way a lift does, that half-inch drop and lift that tells your knees you've arrived. Alex put her hand flat on the wall behind her without deciding to.

The doors opened, and the smell came in first — plastic, hot, everywhere, a wall of it, the whole building's worth — and it got there a full second ahead of the light.
