## 18 · You Came Down

Djan turned round from the locked door with the fist still going on the other side of it. It had slowed down, one blow and then a wait and then another, the way a person knocks once they have stopped expecting anybody to come.

Between two of them, muffled by the door: "I am going to get in so much trouble."

Alex had her back flat against it beside him, Yorick under one arm and the taped hand still on the thumb-turn, as if the lock might decide to undo itself. Hank was on her other side with the Betacam up and the red light on. Nobody was looking at the door any more.

Djan looked at the room.

He tried to have it in one look, the way he had had Level Four in one circuit, and it would not go in. So he measured it with the only instrument he trusted down here. Sixty feet of rope, laid out in his head along the wall to the far corner, end to end, and again, and again. He got five lengths. He ran it the other way and got five again, and he took that for an answer, because it came out the same both ways, and he did not expect anything else in this room to.

Every surface was a screen. The floor. The ceiling, a long way up. The walls, and the square columns that stood out across the floor in rows, and the frame of the door they had just locked, which was playing something on the edge of his eye in a strip as thin as film. There were no seams in any of it. There was not a surface in the place that was not showing him something, and no two of them were showing the same thing, and he watched one long enough to be certain it was not going to go round again. It did not.

The Atrium fountain, going over its lip, tokens turning in the overflow. The Ice Rink, the ice empty and the scoreboard dark. On the nearest column, the four painters at Canvas & Craft, aprons and brushes, going in to a wall together and coming off it together. Whatever they were on now, it was not the door.

Then the ones that were not the mall.

A parking lot, white lines and four cars, one of them theirs. And on the wall to his left, at the height you would hang a notice, the break room at the paper: the counter, the sink with somebody's mug upside down on the drainer, the donated coffee machine with its little orange light on.

"That's been on," Burt said. He had stopped at Djan's shoulder with the backpack hitched high. "That's been on since we left. Nobody turned that off." He said it the way he would have said a gas ring was lit. "That'll have burnt the pot."

Nobody told him it didn't matter.

A column over was Djan's own face. Close, from about four inches under the chin and a little to the left, where his collar was. He had been photographed a few hundred times in his life and could have said where every one of those cameras had stood. Nothing had ever stood there. Nothing could have.

On the far wall, one screen held an empty beach. Sand, low water, a line of foam coming in and going out. No people, no umbrella, no sign. He went through everywhere he had been in this building and everywhere he had been before it, and he did not have it. Nobody said they had it either. Everything else in the room was a place where one of them had stood. He noted it, and did not know what to do with it, and left it there.

Out on the plaza there had been a hum under the ventilation, low in the chest, that he had taken for the building. In here it came clear. It was the Jingle: the little climb, the little fall, the small stop. It had been slowed down until every note had time to sag before the next one came, and moved into a key that made the climb sound like something trying not to cry and the fall like the part where it did. He had heard the Jingle on every Level of this place and it had never once been sad.

He did not care for finding out that it knew how.

---

Jordan was in the middle.

Exactly where the shop screens had put them, upright, one arm raised, small in the glare. From the door it was a figure. They walked.

It took a long time. The floor lit under them a square at a time as they went, and when Djan looked down at the square he had just come off, it was showing him his own sole from underneath: the tread, a grey disc of gum he had not known about, the heel worn down on the outside the way his heels always went. Then the next square. Then the next.

Burt walked on it the way he walked on a roof, flat-footed, testing. Stacy went on her heels as if the floor were ice and had two fingers pressed under her left eye, and took them away when she saw Djan look. Hank had his eye in the cup. Alex's lips moved once, and Djan watched them make five, and then she stopped.

Nobody said anything for the whole length of it.

---

Up close it was Jordan.

The hoodie sleeve on the left side had ridden up past the elbow and stayed there, and the arm under it was plastic to the shoulder. Pale, without a hair on it, and nothing in the knuckles to say they had ever bent. It went up the side of the neck and stopped at the jaw in a line, as straight as a seam on a moulding, and there was a crack along the line the width of a hair.

One side of the face was Jordan's. The other side was going smooth, and a little clear, like a mannequin that has stood in a shop window through a summer. The glasses were still on. On the plastic side, the arm of them rested on an ear that had lost its folds.

The right arm was up at forty-five degrees, elbow high, and the camcorder was at the end of it. Djan looked for where the hand stopped and the camcorder began. He found the mole on the back of the hand, Jordan's mole, a quarter of an inch from the grip strap. Past the strap he could not find anything. The skin went under and the housing came out, and somewhere in between there was a place where it was both, and he could not put his eye on it.

The little red light was on. It was pointed at them. Hank's red light was pointed back.

The eyes were Jordan's. They went across the five of them and did not stop on anybody.

The lips were moving. It took Djan a moment to understand that they were moving in time, and another to understand with what.

"Come on down… to Green Dawn… where the savings never… never…" Jordan sang it very low, in the slow minor of the room.

He had never heard it with words. He noted that once, and put it with the beach.

Then, underneath the singing, in a different voice, lower, not carrying, a voice that was only Jordan's: "…don't develop the tape without me."

Djan got it because he had been listening to the singing for what was missing from it, and this was what was missing. Nobody else turned. Stacy was looking at the face. Burt was looking at the feet. Hank was looking at the picture in the eyepiece, which was not the same thing.

---

Something in his inside pocket went warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a mug is when somebody has just put it into your hands and kept their own hands round it a second longer than they needed to.

He knew what it was without touching it. He had carried them since Level Two, flat against his ribs, since a woman in PageTurn had put them in his hand on her way out of the door because she was going to let go of them either way. Grey. No image coming up, none going out, nothing on the borders. He had looked at them in a lift and on an ice rink and in an art supply store, and they had been grey every time, and he had never once told anybody what they were. He had not known. A thing he did not know was a thing he kept.

He took the stack out. It was still grey.

He could have looked first. That was what he did; he looked first, and then he decided who else got to. He squared the stack against his palm and held it up level with Jordan's eyes, the faces turned toward Jordan and the white backs toward himself, so that for the first time in his working life somebody else was going to see the picture before he did.

Jordan's eyes came down to the grey squares and stayed there.

The top one went brown. Then colour came in from the edges, all four sides at once, closing toward the middle, the way a print comes up in the tray but faster, and Djan turned his wrist an inch so he could see it over the top. The colour met in the centre. It did that as if it had been waiting for somebody to look.

He dealt them off the top one at a time and held each one up, and Jordan's eyes went to each.

The newsroom. Not a newsroom; theirs, with the water-stained ceiling tile over the second desk that everybody had been meaning to report.

A row of teacups on a desk, each with a strip of tape on it and a name in small cramped print. The J's had a hook. He would have known that hand anywhere. He had ruled out two notes in this building on that hook.

A parking lot at the end of a day, the light going long and gold across the white lines.

A dog asleep on somebody's porch, one board in the step missing.

A plate of food somebody had made at home. You could tell from the edges.

A sunrise over a freeway.

The five of them, outside the office, squinting into the sun. Burt with a hand up for a visor. Stacy halfway through a word. Hank with the camera down at his side. Djan himself at the end of the row, looking straight at whoever was taking it. He did not remember anybody taking it. He remembered every photograph anybody had ever taken of him.

None of them were of the mall.

They had never been hers, he thought. That was a guess. He had nothing to put under it, and he did not say it.

Jordan's eyes went from the dog to the plate to the freeway, and came to the last one, and stayed. Then Jordan blinked. Hard, the whole face in it, the way a person blinks stepping out of a dark room into daylight.

The Jingle stopped.

Not in the room. The screens went on with whatever they went on with. It stopped in Djan's chest, where it had been sitting since the plaza, the way a string stops when somebody lays a flat hand on it.

"You came down," Jordan said.

The voice was Jordan's, all of it, and it was quick, and it went up at the end. Djan had heard that voice come across the office on a Thursday afternoon with a question in it nobody had wanted to answer. The eyes went round them now, and stopped on each of them in turn, and knew each one.

"How long has it been?"

Djan went for the number, and there was no number there.

He knew how many Levels. He knew how many escalators, and how many lengths of rope this room was, and how many photographs were in his hand. He went looking for the other thing and found the light, which had not changed once since the lift, and nothing else.

Alex had counted everything since the stairs in the annex. She opened her mouth, and shut it. Hank took his eye out of the cup. Stacy looked at Burt, and Burt looked at the floor.

Nobody had counted. Not one of them had been willing to be the first.

---

Before anybody had to, Jordan swayed.

The knees went. The plastic arm could not come out to catch anything, and the right arm would not come down, and Jordan started to go over sideways like a thing with nothing to hold it. They all reached at once. Djan's hand got the hoodie at the shoulder. That was when he saw the feet.

Jordan's sneakers were going smooth. The same pale plastic as the arm, coming up out of the floor screen into the canvas, into the laces, which had gone flat and glossy and were no longer laces, and up past the ankle bone. It looked like the floor had made up its mind. The crack at the jaw crept a little nearer the ear.

Every screen in the room flickered at once, and when they came back they were all showing the same thing.

Djan looked up. The ceiling, the walls, every column, the strip in the door frame: Jordan, from above, standing very still, and five people around them with their hands out, from above. Thousands of them. The one in the middle not moving.

"I'm sorry," Jordan said. It had gone thin again. "I'm so tired. I haven't — I don't think I've eaten. Is that a thing I still do?"

Burt already had the backpack off.

He had it down on the screens and the zip open before Djan saw him decide, and out of it came the paper sack, the snack pack Burt had made at home the night before they came in. He had offered it at the top of every flight of stairs in the annex and on every Level since. He had offered it to the circle in the vacant unit, and nobody there had taken anything either.

"Somebody in that circle said you didn't eat," Burt said. He opened the top of the sack.

Djan could not have said what anything in this place smelled like any more. Plastic, and under that turpentine, and under that nothing. This smelled like a kitchen on a Sunday evening with the radio on.

Burt took out a square of something, cut by hand, the edges not even, and held it up to Jordan's mouth the way you feed somebody who is working on a roof and can't let go of what they're holding.

Jordan took it. Chewed. Stopped moving.

"Someone made this," Jordan said. "Someone just made this."

One tear went down the side of the face that was still Jordan's. It did not go down the other side.

---

It went back like a tide.

Colour in the cheek first, on the side that had it. Then the colour spread toward the jaw, and the crack along the jaw began to close from the ear end, a millimetre and then another. Djan had watched a wall heal on Level Four with Hank's drag mark in it, and this was the same speed, and it was going the other way. The plastic slid back down the neck. It went under the neckline of the hoodie and stopped somewhere below the collarbone, where he could not see it.

The ankles softened. Then the heels. The laces came up out of the gloss one eyelet at a time and were laces.

Jordan's sneakers were just sneakers.

Every screen in the room glitched together, and for half a second all of them showed the same dog asleep on the same porch. Djan had the print in his hand. Same porch. Same board missing from the step.

Then the mall again. The fountain, the rink, the painters, the coffee machine with its light on.

Jordan's knees went again, and Burt took the weight. He did not lift so much as gather; he got one arm round under Jordan's shoulders and held them up the way he would carry a bag of groceries up a flight of steps, high against his chest so nothing fell out of the top.

Djan took the rope off his shoulder, ran a loop round Jordan's waist, and tied it off to Burt's belt, because the floor had wanted Jordan's feet once already.

Nobody said anything about it. Burt looked down at the knot and nodded.

"Okay," Jordan said. Steadier. They found the floor with both feet and stood on it. "Okay. I'm here. I'm really here."

They looked up at the man holding them, the hard hat and the backpack and the face that had been counting heads since the parking lot.

"Hi, Burt."

The right arm was still up at forty-five degrees. Still part of the camera. And the little red light was still on, and it was pointed at Djan.
