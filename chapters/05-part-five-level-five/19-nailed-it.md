## 19 · Nailed It

> *PLEASE STAND BY. We are experiencing technical difficulties. Do not adjust your set. Normal programming will resume shortly.*

Stacy watched Jordan look down at his own right hand the way you look at a coat somebody has left on your chair. Burt had him up under one arm, the rope from Djan's knot running from Jordan's waist to Burt's belt, and Jordan's sneakers were sneakers again, and the arm was still up at its angle with the camcorder on the end of it and the little red light going.

Her headache sat behind the left eye. It had come on at the top of the last escalator and had not moved since. The voice had come with it — far off, level, a list in a tone she could get and words she couldn't — and since Alex threw the lock it had been getting closer, a step at a time, like somebody walking toward her down a corridor with a clipboard.

Behind them, a fist on the door. Then nothing for a while. Then the fist.

"It won't come off," Jordan said. "I tried. The first day I tried so hard." He looked at the hand, and the hand did not look back. "If you pull on it, something breaks, and it isn't the camera."

Nobody said anything. Burt shifted his grip, gently, the way you shift a sleeping kid.

Jordan swallowed. "But I can't walk out of here holding it. Every time I get near a door it turns me around. It wants to keep filming."

The red light blinked.

So that was the job. Not getting Jordan out of the room — Burt could carry Jordan out of any room on earth. Getting the room off Jordan.

---

Alex had her working face on, the one that added things up. Hank had the Betacam on his shoulder with its own red light lit, and his mouth shut tight. Djan was looking at the floor, where a screen under Jordan's sneakers was showing the underside of Jordan's sneakers.

"Then we'll carry the both of you," Burt said. "You and the camera."

"It turns me around, Burt."

"It can turn me around, then. I'm bigger."

Under the ventilation the Jingle went round in its minor key, slow, the stop at the end running long. It started again.

And the voice arrived.

It was clear now, close enough to hear the breath in it, and it was not the mall's.

It was her own voice.

Not the one she used for filling air, and not the one she used on the phone to a booker. The other one — the one with the count in its ear, the one that had stood in car parks and on courthouse steps and never in six years missed a mark. It was saying one thing, and saying it again, the way you say a line to yourself in a lift on the way up.

*You've done this before.*

She had no idea what she'd done before. She stood there with her bag on her shoulder and her heels on a screen showing a fountain, and she understood that she was not going to be told.

That was fine. She'd worked with producers like that.

---

"Hank," she said. "Out of my shot."

"I'm getting this."

"You're getting my left side. Go round."

He went round. That was the thing about Hank: he would argue with a wall, but he would never argue about an angle.

She stepped in close, square to the camcorder, the little lens two feet from her face. The red light looked at her. She looked at it back, and found her mark, and put her weight on the front foot.

"Honey," she said.

It came out in the voice. It came out warm and round and pitched to reach a sofa in somebody's living room, and Alex, at the edge of her eye, actually took a step back.

"Honey. Look at you. You've been working three days straight." She had no idea if it was three. Three is a number that sounds like a lot on television. "Three days! No breaks, no lunch. That's not dedication, honey, that's a cry for help. You need a break. I'm calling your agent."

The red light blinked.

"And while we wait for him to pick up," she said, turning a few degrees, giving it the good side, "let me ask you something. Are you tired? Run down? Do you wake up feeling like you're made of something other than you?" She put a hand flat to her own chest. "I was. Until I found this. New formula. B12-infused. Clinically shown to — honey, are you listening to me?"

The red light blinked again. She would have sworn on anything you like that it blinked slower.

And Jordan's mouth moved.

Only at one corner, only a quarter of an inch, and only for a second. But Stacy had stood in a shop on Level Four putting wrong guesses in front of four painters and got nothing back off any of them, not once, and she knew the quarter-inch when it came. Faces leak. This one had.

So she kept going.

---

"Good evening," she said, and the voice dropped half a register and squared its shoulders, and she was at a desk with a sheet of paper she was never going to look at. "Our top story tonight. A local newspaper, eleven staff, a circulation you could seat in a car park, sends its intern to look into a rumor at an office building by the old mall."

Jordan's eyes came to her. His own eyes, wet at the rims.

"The intern does not come back. Five members of staff go up to the fourth floor after him and step into a lift that is not on the fire plan." A beat for the graphic. "They have since visited a bookshop that only takes returns, and an ice rink where nobody has ever scored. On Level Four they were offered a door to the car park." She let that sit, the way you let a number sit. "They closed it."

Burt made a sound in his chest and turned it into a cough.

"Sources describe the coffee as nonexistent."

"Stacy," Alex said, very quietly.

"And tonight," Stacy said, "in a dramatic development, we go live."

Her right hand was in the Neverfull.

She had not decided to put it there. It had gone in on its own, the way it went in when you were on air and the autocue died. You never looked down. You talked, and you kept your eyes on the glass, and your hands went and did whatever the problem was, and if you were good nobody ever knew there had been one.

She was good.

"Our correspondent is reaching into her bag," she told the camcorder. "She is not looking down. Viewers at home will note that she never looks down."

Her fingers went through it by feel. The makeup bag, zipped. The grip of the drill, rubber, cold, enormous. The deck was under the drill, and it stayed there. Past that, a coil of flex, smooth, a plug with two flat pins.

*You've done this before.*

Her hand closed on the curling iron.

And there was the rink. The cold coming off the hand a foot away, the fingers locked shut round a drill. Kneeling on a mat in this skirt with the iron on the laminate, and moving it a quarter of an inch at a time, and not pushing, and saying *there you go* to a hand that belonged to nobody.

Heat and patience. That was all it had been. She had done this before.

"We are getting reports," she said, "of an outlet."

There was one at the foot of the nearest column, set low in the one square inch of that column not showing anything, because there always is. She went down onto one heel without breaking eye contact with the lens and found it with the pins first and pushed home, and the little light on the barrel came on orange at the edge of her vision, the only orange thing in the room.

"It takes a minute, folks," she said. "We're going to take that minute."

---

She did not touch the camcorder. She took Jordan's wrist in her left hand, bare, because it was a wrist, and it was cool and thin and ran with a pulse she could feel against two fingers. Then she tested the barrel against her own forearm, the way you test an iron, and laid it across the seam where Jordan's fingers stopped and the housing began, and held it there, and did not push.

"Our correspondent is applying heat," she said. "Gently. She'd like it noted she has never in her career burned anybody who didn't have it coming."

Jordan winced. Then the whole of him went loose against Burt's side, and a long breath came out of him, longer than a person ought to have in them.

"Oh," Jordan said. "That's warm." His eyes shut. "I forgot what warm was."

"Our intern," Stacy told the lens, "is reporting warm."

She moved the barrel a quarter of an inch. She waited. She moved it again.

The plastic at the seam went glossy first, and then soft, the way a crayon goes on a dashboard in August — not running, just giving up its edges. A smell came off it, hot and sweet, and she breathed through her mouth and kept talking. She did traffic on the escalators. She gave the camcorder a five-day forecast of more light, and nobody laughed, and nobody stopped her.

The first finger came away from the housing. Then the second, slower, the soft plastic drawing out between them in a thread that thinned and let go.

It did not sound like plastic. It sounded like a very small bell, struck once.

The camcorder slipped out of Jordan's hand.

The Neverfull was already open on her hip. She had not opened it — it had been open since she went in for the iron, and it had held a drill out of a dead man's grip without complaint, and now it took this. The camcorder dropped into the mouth of it and went down out of sight, red light and all, and the zip ran shut along its track on its own. She had not touched it.

---

Jordan's right hand was free.

It was pink, and wrinkled at the fingertips like a hand that has been in the bath much too long, and it shook. On the back of it, under the little finger, there was a brown mark the size of a match head. Stacy knew whose that was. Djan had told them, at a counter in the cold, and none of them had forgotten it.

Jordan flexed the fingers and watched them do it. Then he laughed — one surprised bark of it — and then he was crying, and then it was both, the way it is at airports.

Burt said, "There you go," and did not let go of him.

The left arm stayed what it was. Smooth and pale to the shoulder, hanging off him, a thing that had been put on rather than grown. Stacy looked at it once and saw Alex look at it once, and everybody in that room could tell, without a word passed round, that it was not coming back.

Jordan looked at it too. Then at the five of them. Then he shrugged, with the shoulder that still worked.

"I'll take it," he said.

*Nailed it,* said the voice in her head, and it sounded pleased.

Then it was quiet. She waited for it the way you wait for the count in your ear at the end of a piece, and it didn't come, and behind her left eye there was nothing. The headache was gone. She had not noticed how loud it had been until it wasn't.

She stood there with an iron in her hand and its light still on, and the first thing she thought — and she would never tell any of them this — was the lining.

It was a Neverfull. It had a lining. And something that had been fused to a person was lying in it now, on top of her makeup bag, with its red light going in the dark.

"Stace," Hank said, from behind the Betacam. "That was—"

"Don't," she said. "Don't say it on tape."

---

The mall noticed.

The Jingle stopped, and the room went silent for one full second.

It was the first true silence Stacy had heard since the lift. No piano. No ventilation. Nothing at all except, behind them, the fist on the door, once, and Jordan's breathing.

Then every screen in the room changed at once.

Thousands of them. The floor and the ceiling and the walls and the columns and the frame of the door they had locked, the fountain and the rink and the painters and the break room with the coffee machine — all of it gone, and every one of them showing the same thing.

Them. The six of them, from every angle at once. From above, small and close together. From below, through the glass under their own feet, up the inside of Burt's trouser legs. And close — closer than any lens has a right to be — Hank's eye at the viewfinder, Alex's taped knuckles, Djan's mouth, Jordan's wet face, one long crease down it where the plastic had been.

Her own nose. On a screen at the foot of a column, eighteen inches wide, she could see the pores on her own nose.

She was a professional, and her first reaction was a professional one, and she would not have repeated it to anybody.

Then the Jingle came back.

Not slow. Not in a minor key. At full speed and full volume, out of every screen in the room at once — out of tinny speakers and out of clean ones, bright and cheerful and not very good — and it had the words now, the ones Jordan had been singing, and they were for everybody.

COME ON DOWN, TO GREEN DAWN, WHERE THE SAVINGS NEVER END —

The floor shook with it. She felt it come up through the screen into her heels and up her shins and into her teeth. Burt had both arms round Jordan now. Alex was saying something with her whole face and no sound came out of it.

Stacy opened her mouth and hummed.

The door tune. The one from the art shop, with more notes in it than the piano had, the one that had put brass on a painted wall and opened a car park for thirty seconds. Somebody had to try it, and it was hers.

She hummed it as loud as she had ever hummed anything. She could feel it in the bones under her ears and in the back of her nose, and she could not hear one note of it. It went into the Jingle like a coin into the fountain, and the Jingle closed over it and went on.

She hummed it again, louder, and got nothing back, and thought — she could not have proved it, and did not try — that the mall was doing this on purpose.

COME ON DOWN —

The Neverfull jerked against her hip.

She looked down. She had not looked down once, all the way through, and now she did. The bag kicked again, hard, the canvas bulging at one end and then the other, the strap sawing at her shoulder through her blouse. Something in it wanted out. On the screens, from above and below and close enough to see the grain of the canvas, a thousand bags thrashed on a thousand shoulders, and a thousand of her got both arms round one and held on.

TO GREEN DAWN —

She held on. The iron swung on its flex and knocked against her shin, still hot.

And behind them, at the door — where the whole time there had been a fist, one every few seconds, steady as a clock, going on and on about how much trouble somebody was going to be in — there was nothing.
