## 15 · Closed to the Public

> *SCREENING ROOM — CLOSED TO THE PUBLIC. For your safety and convenience this area is monitored at all times. Please enjoy the Food Court, now open on every Level.*

Djan walked half a step behind Alex up the length of Level Five, because she had said it, and it had taken her four words, and nobody had argued. He was the only one of them looking at the ceiling.

It was a screen, like everything else here. Ten feet up, edge to edge, no grid in it and no vent, and the light came out of the picture and not from anywhere behind it. Under his shoes the floor showed a corridor with a bench in it. He walked across the bench.

What he wanted was the floor, whole, before it moved. Every Level they had come into had been one thing from the foot of the escalator and another thing a hundred feet on, and for four Levels he had got the second version late. So: the doors. And on the way to the doors, all of it, once, properly, while it was still the shape it had been when they stepped off.

---

No fountain. Nothing in the middle at all — no easel, no platform turning a car round under a spotlight. On every Level so far something had stood in the centre of the floor and asked to be looked at, and here there was more screen, and Burt's boots one stride to his right.

Two shops were open. *Télévision Libertés*, in gold letters across the glass, the same lettering as Level One down to the accents. The bell over its door had been wired back with a twist of copper so it could not ring. Next to it, the unit with brown paper over the glass and no sign above it. Its door stood open the width of a hand, and a strip of low yellow light lay out across the floor in front of it, the way light lies under a door in a house where somebody is still up.

The shoe shop stayed dark. In its window the nearest pair was a brogue and a child's sandal, laced to each other.

One short corridor led off to the left. A bench, a bin, nothing at the end. A place to sit, if anyone had been sitting.

And the screens. At the escalator foot they had shown the rest of the building — the fountain, the ice, eleven escalators. Out here, past some line he had not seen them cross, they showed only this. This plaza, from high in the corners and low at the skirting, with nobody in it. On one the brown-papered door was shut. On another a man in a short-sleeved shirt pushed a floor polisher across the middle of the plaza, slowly, and the cord went off the edge of the frame. The light in all of them was the light in here, so there was no telling what hour any of them had been taken at, only that it was not this one.

Djan looked at the man with the polisher longest. He was the only person on any of them.

Then the one that was not like the others.

It was set into the centre of the ceiling, larger than the rest, tilted a few degrees toward the doors, so that from the escalator you would have had its edge and nothing else. Hank stopped under it with his chin up.

"Hey," Hank said. "That's us."

It was. Five people from above, foreshortened to the tops of their heads and the points of their shoulders. Burt's hard hat a yellow coin. The part in Stacy's hair. Djan found himself as a man with his face tipped all the way back and a camera on a strap against his chest, who looked like somebody who had lost a thing on a roof.

Hank waved. On the ceiling a small man with a Betacam on his shoulder waved back.

Stacy laughed, one note through her nose, two fingers pressed to her temple where the headache had been sitting since the escalator. "Hi, Hank."

"Hi," Hank said, to the ceiling, and waved again, and was waved at again. "That's a clean feed. There's no lag on that at all."

"Live," Alex said, and Djan watched her put it on whatever list she kept, near the bottom, under curiosities. Then she looked toward the doors, and he looked with her, and the woman was there.

---

He'd had her since the escalator, a vertical shape in front of the grey doors, and had not let himself have her properly, because he had wanted the room first.

Fifty feet. A green blazer, the shoulders built up square, buttoned once. Skirt to the knee. Flat shoes, the toes together. Her hands folded at her waist, right over left. On the lapel a small gold pin: a disc with eight rays round it. He had seen it on the cover of a brochure and on Hank's magnet and on five green ovals, and it was on the back of his own wrist.

She was watching them. He could tell the way you can tell a stranger on a platform has clocked you — in the small adjustments. As they came up the plaza her shoulders squared to them a degree at a time, and her head came round with them. Not her eyes. Her head was tipped back and turned a little out, chin up, the way a woman stands in a station reading the departures, and her eyes were on whatever was up there, and they stayed there.

Twenty feet out from the doors a line of yellow floor tape ran across the plaza, laid flat over the screens, the picture going on underneath it. Alex stopped a stride short of the tape and the rest of them stopped behind her.

"Welcome to the Screening Room!" the woman said. "I'm afraid this area is closed to the public. Why don't you head over to the Food Court?"

Every sentence went up at the end, like a question it already knew the answer to. She said it to the air over their heads, smiling.

"We're looking for a friend," Hank said.

"Okay?" Still to the air. "And?"

---

They went at it the way five professionals go at a locked door: in order, and each of them sure their way was the obvious one.

"We work here," Alex said.

Burt was already stepping up beside her. He put one finger to the brim of the hard hat. "Call-out on the ductwork back there, ma'am. Air handling. You've got no return on this whole floor. We're the ticket."

There was no ticket.

"I'm Employee of the Month," Hank said.

The woman's smile did not go anywhere. "So, I may be a vapid, light-hearted, airy-voiced girl," she said, "but I do not appreciate being lied to. You're obviously customers. I can see your tattoos."

Djan counted them where they were.

Stacy's was under her collar. Alex's was on the inside of her forearm under a sleeve she had pulled down on Level Four and kept down. Burt's was on the point of his shoulder, under his shirt. Hank's was on the back of his calf, under a sock, under a trouser leg, inside a boot. His own was on the back of his left wrist, and both his hands were in his jacket pockets.

She had not looked down once.

"Also?" she said. "My coworker upstairs called down to say you were coming."

Nobody asked which coworker. Djan thought of a man in a waistcoat with his smile left exactly where it had been, and the voice coming down the escalator after them from further away than it should have been.

---

Burt got the paper sack out of the backpack.

He opened the top and held it out across the tape at arm's length, the way you offer a sandwich to a dog you have not been introduced to. "Spinach puffs," he said. "Made 'em myself. There's plenty."

"Oh my gosh," the woman said, to the ceiling. "No? Thank you, though?"

Burt looked into the sack as though it might have done something to deserve that, and folded the top over, and put it away.

Alex had her coat open. She came out with the cash — a fan of real notes, soft with being carried — and in the other hand, the taped one, a palm of brass tokens with the leaf side up. She held both out over the tape and let the woman not look at them.

"What use do I have for money?" the woman said. "I've been here for three hundred years."

She said it the way somebody says their age at a party when they have stopped minding.

Alex kept her hands where they were. "Do you want out?"

"No." Pleasantly. "I have a good job. Please let me do my job and back away."

Djan took his hands out of his pockets.

"Are you hiring?" he said.

"No new employees." The head tipped another degree toward the ceiling. "We're perfectly staffed."

He put his hands back. He had known how it would come out while he was still asking. It had been worth asking for the shape of the answer — perfectly, said like a word on a badge.

"Our friend got in," Hank said. "Blonde. Glasses. Old camera. So either you let him in or you didn't do a very good job."

"Sure!" she said. "Come and try."

---

Hank tried.

He did not discuss it. He stepped over the tape the way he had stepped into every room since the lift, first, his shoulder already turned for the gap, and he got one stride in.

She moved her right hand. It went back past her hip and flicked the blazer open and settled on something at her belt, and stayed there, the fingers round it. Her eyes did not come down. Her chin did not come down.

Hank stopped with his weight on the front foot.

"My one job," the woman said, brightly, to nobody, "which I have happily performed for the last three hundred years, has been to keep idiots like you from approaching the room I'm meant to guard."

Hank stepped back over the tape. He did it slowly, the way you get off ice.

"What are you looking at," Alex said. Not a question, the way she said it.

"Why are you still standing here?"

"That's very helpful," Alex said, to the rest of them, low. "What is she looking *at*."

"Follow her eyes," Hank said, and did, and stood with his face up toward the lintel. "There's nothing. She's looking at the top of the door."

Stacy had her eyes shut against the headache. "Some people look up," she said.

---

Burt lowered himself onto the floor.

He did it in stages, as a big man does — one knee, then the other, then a hand flat on the screen, then down onto his forearms and his belly, with the backpack still on and riding up between his shoulder blades. The hard hat tipped forward and he pushed it back with one knuckle. Under his chest a corridor went on being empty.

"She's looking up," he said, to the floor, reasonably. "I'll go under."

Nobody stopped him.

Burt began to crawl. Elbow, knee, elbow. He hummed while he did it, low and in time with the elbows — four plucked notes and a rest, the music from a heist film, the part where the man in the black polo neck comes down on a wire. He kept it up the whole way. He was not doing it for a laugh. He was doing it, Djan was nearly sure, the way he hummed on a ladder.

The woman's chin stayed up. Her eyes stayed up.

Her head came round.

Not down. Round. It turned on her neck a degree for every foot of floor Burt covered, smoothly, keeping pace with him exactly, the way a security camera in a car park keeps pace with a car, and when he stopped to get the backpack off his kidneys, it stopped.

Djan looked up.

The ceiling screen was there, in the centre, where he had left it. Five people from above, and a strip of yellow tape, and a woman in a green blazer standing in front of two grey doors. One of the five was flat on the floor and creeping toward the tape, a hard hat moving across the picture as plain as a coin across a table. The woman's face was turned up into the lens, small and pale and smiling. When the hard hat moved, her face moved.

He looked down at her. Up at the screen. Down at her. Up.

Burt went another foot. On the ceiling, the face went with him.

That was all it was. He had it. He put it where he put things, behind his breastbone, with a door in a corridor on Level Three and a set of grey squares in his jacket pocket that he had not looked at since PageTurn.

Alex was next to him with her arms folded. "What is she looking at," she said, to herself, to the side of his head.

Djan watched the hard hat on the ceiling, and said nothing.

Burt reached the tape. He lay with his chin an inch off it, and the woman spoke to the air above him.

"Are we doing this?"

Burt considered the tape at close range. "Floor's level," he said, after a while. "Somebody did a good job on this floor."

He got up the way he had got down, in stages, and dusted his knees though there was nothing on them, and came back.

---

"Okay," Stacy said, with her eyes still shut. "Why not just — why not, though? Talking didn't work."

Alex had the taped hand open at her side. She closed it, and opened it, and the silver caught the light off the floor. Djan had seen that hand knock a book out of his own grip on Level Two.

"Plan B," Alex said.

"And if the door's locked?" Burt said.

"Then we're on the wrong side of it with her."

Hank was at the tape again. He did not step over it this time. He planted himself there with the Betacam on his shoulder and his boots square, and looked back at the four of them, and did not step back.

The baton came out in one movement. Her hand went to the belt and came round in front of her with a black grip in it and her wrist snapped once, and three sections of steel shot out of each other to their full length with a sound like a drawer slammed shut, and she held it down along her leg, the tip an inch off the screen. Her chin had not moved.

Djan looked at the hand.

He had seen hands like it on the old men who fished off the harbour wall, below where he stacked stones on weekends. The knuckles had gone square. The tendons stood in the back of it like cables under a tarp. She held the grip with no effort in it at all, the fingers closed in the order fingers close when they have done it more times than anything else. It was the hand of a person who had done one thing for a very long time, and had got as good at it as anybody can.

"Okay," Hank said, without turning his head. "This is the one at the end." He took a step back from the tape. "And we are not ready."

Nobody argued with that either.

They backed off together, all five, the way you back off a dog. The woman stood her baton on its tip and closed it against her flat palm, section into section, one push, and slid it back under the blazer, and folded her hands again at her waist, right over left.

"Mama didn't raise no fool," she said, to the ceiling, warmly.

---

They walked back down the plaza toward the brown-papered door and the light under it, because it was the only thing on Level Five standing open. Stacy had both hands at her temples. Hank had the camera down at his hip.

Alex dropped back half a step until she was level with Djan.

"Djan."

"Yeah."

"What's she looking at."

She said it to him and nobody else, directly, in the voice she used when she had already asked a thing and was asking it once more for the record.

He had it ready. It was four words, as it happened; he had counted them.

"Lovely hand on her," Burt said, from the front. "That's a working hand. You don't get that in an office."

Alex looked at the back of Burt's head and let out a breath through her nose, and the question went by, and Djan let it.

He looked up.

On the ceiling screen, five small people were walking away from a woman in a green blazer, down a plaza made of pictures of itself. Four of them had their heads down, or forward, or turned to one another. One of them was looking up, at him.

Behind them, small, at the far edge of the frame, the woman's head was tilted up too.

He did not tell anyone.
