## 16 · The Ones Who Stopped

> *THIS UNIT IS AVAILABLE. Ideal for a family business. Flexible terms. Long-term tenants welcome. Please inquire within.*

The papered door was standing an inch off its frame, and Burt Calloway pushed it the rest of the way with two fingers, because a door left like that on a site is either a man inside or a hazard, and both want looking at.

His knees still had the floor in them. He had gone the width of that plaza on his belly in front of four people and a woman in a green blazer, humming, and she had turned her head with him the whole way like a sprinkler on a timer and had never once looked down. He was mostly fine about it. A thing you try that doesn't work is still a thing off the list. She was down at the far end in front of those doors, and they needed another way past her, and Burt was the one who went toward things.

"Burt," Alex said, behind him.

"Just looking."

---

The light in there came off the floor.

A camping lantern, green tank, two mantles, the kind a man buys for one weekend and keeps in the garage for twenty years. It was the first light Burt had seen in this building that came from something.

Around it, cross-legged on bare concrete, there were people. He counted them, because he counted. Nine. Nobody was talking. A few were looking at the lantern. Most were looking at nothing, the way a crew looks at the wall of the break trailer on its third double, and one of them, a man near the door with his tie loosened, lifted his head and looked at the five of them standing in the light from the plaza. Looked at them the way you look at rain through a window. Then he put his eyes back on the floor.

The brown paper was taped over the glass from the inside. Slatwall, chrome hooks, nothing on them. It smelled of plastic in here too, and under that, faintly, of people.

"Hi," Alex said. "Does anybody know how to get into the Screening Room?"

Nobody answered her. The lantern made a small sound, steady.

Stacy crouched at the edge of the circle in the heels, which took some doing, and started talking in the voice she used on people who'd had a long day. Burt stayed in the door. He had seen this on crews. Not the ones who quit — quitting was a thing a man did. These were the ones still on the job who had stopped expecting to go home off it. Nobody here was hurt. The door had been open.

"Oh," somebody said. "It's you."

She was on the far side of the lantern. He knew her by the coat before he knew her face, and he knew her face because it had been pressed into his shirt, up on Level Two, while she thanked him in an accent he couldn't follow and he patted her back three times with the pack still on.

She wasn't crying. He looked for it, and it wasn't there.

"Ma'am," Burt said.

"You did the—" She made a small push with one hand, the book going. She smiled at him, warm, the way you smile at a man you met at a wedding. "You were very kind."

"How'd you get down here?"

She had tried, she said. Once she had found a staircase and gone up it, and it had come out somewhere she hadn't been. She'd got lost. She said that part plainly, like it was the name of a street. "I don't know how to leave," she said. "I tried, and I don't know how." Then, with her arms crossed tight over the coat: "But I'm okay. I think I'm okay here."

Her arms were still doing what they'd done on Level Two. He had said so then, out loud, and nobody had had a coat going spare.

Behind him, Djan had come up into the doorway. Burt didn't turn around. He saw it in the edge of his eye — Djan's hand going flat against the front of his jacket, over the inside pocket, and staying there. The woman looked straight at Djan and smiled the same smile, and didn't ask him for anything, and Djan didn't offer.

"You could come on with us," Burt said.

"Oh, no." She shook her head at him, kindly, as if he'd offered her his seat on a bus. "No. It's nice here, by the light."

Burt got the paper sack out of the backpack.

He went round the circle with it, one person at a time, because you don't skip anybody, and he held it out to her too, and nobody took anything.

"The other one didn't eat either," a woman said, from the far side.

Burt folded the top down. "Well," he said. "Offer stands."

He put it away.

Alex had stayed standing. "We're looking for someone," she said. "Glasses. Fair hair. A camera they don't put down."

The man with the tie answered, still looking at the concrete. "Three days ago," he said. "Came down the escalator with it in their hand. Walked out there." His chin moved, very slightly, toward the plaza and the far end of it. "That's all."

"Did they get past her?"

"Don't know."

"Did anybody see how?"

"Don't know," he said again, and that was the whole of it. Nobody in the circle looked up. Burt looked at nine people who had been here long enough to put a lantern in the middle and sit round it, and he could not have told you one thing wrong with any of them except that they had stopped.

"Come on," Alex said.

He went, last, and he left the door the way he'd found it, an inch off the frame.

---

Télévision Libertés was across the plaza, in the same gold letters as upstairs, with a bell over the door that somebody had wired open so it couldn't ring.

There was a neon sign in the window. It came on and went off and came on, slow, and then a run of quicker ones, and then dark a while, and started again.

Alex watched it go round twice.

"That's Morse," she said. "S. C. R. E. E. N. S. It was doing that upstairs."

"You didn't say."

"I didn't read it upstairs."

Inside, it was the same shop — the counter, the room going back further than the front had any business allowing, CRT sets racked three high, and newer flat ones bolted into the gaps like somebody had been patching. Same bench under the counter, where a woman had come out with a screwdriver and a bundle of cable over her wrist.

There was no woman.

There was a mannequin behind the counter. Plain plastic, no face to speak of, the joins showing at the shoulder and the wrist. On its head it had a purple felt bowler.

It turned to face them when they came in.

Not fast. A quarter turn, at the base, the way a heavy thing turns when someone's leaning on it. Then it stayed, pointed at the five of them.

"That's her hat," Stacy said.

Nobody said anything else about the hat.

---

Every screen in the shop was showing the same thing, and Burt's eyes had come off it once, for the mannequin, and gone straight back.

A room. Screens on every wall of it, floor to ceiling, edge to edge, and every one of those screens lit. In the middle of it, a person standing with one arm up. The hand at the top of the arm had a camera in it. Glasses. Fair hair gone white in that light. Not moving.

On the biggest set, down in the bottom corner, the numbers were running. Burt went and looked. They were not fifteen minutes behind. They were not an hour behind. They said now.

"That's live," Hank said. "That's — Alex, that's live."

"I know."

"That's them."

"I know, Hank."

Burt looked at the arm.

He had held drywall over his head while another man got screws into it, and he knew what that was in the shoulder after one minute, and after three. Nobody held an arm straight up longer than that. On every screen in the room Jordan had it up there like it was resting.

Stacy had her hand over her mouth. Djan stood very still by the door. Alex had her face a foot off the largest screen and was not saying anything.

That was six. He counted them, five and one, and the one was on the wall.

"Somebody ought to get that kid a chair," Burt said.

---

The mannequin was trying.

It couldn't talk and it couldn't walk, but it could rock on the base, and it was rocking, small and quick, the way a dog does at a door. Then it leaned. It leaned out over the counter so far that Burt took a step toward it to catch it, and it stopped at an angle nothing should stop at, and held there, pointing with its whole body at the countertop.

There was a tape on the counter. A Betacam cassette, taped down flat with a strip of clear packing tape.

"That's mine," Hank said. "That's my format."

He got it up with a thumbnail and loaded it, and put his eye to the viewfinder and kept it there.

"Blank," he said.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing on it. It's new." He did not take it out. He shut the door on it, and something went across his face that Burt didn't have a word for. "Okay," Hank said. "Then I'm getting all of it. From here. Every second. When we're out somebody's going to say prove it."

The mannequin straightened up. It turned again, slow, on its base, until it was pointing out through the shop window and across the plaza at the far end, at the doors.

Then it turned back.

---

"Okay," Alex said. She came round the counter end and stood in front of it like she was interviewing it. "Yes or no. Can you nod?"

It bobbed. Once, from the base, the whole body.

"Is that yes?"

It bobbed.

"Do you know where Jordan is?"

It bobbed.

"Is there a way into the Screening Room?"

It bounced on its base like a kid holding a secret, and swung round, and pointed itself — the whole stiff length of it, the hat brim and all — at Burt.

Everybody looked at Burt.

"Well, I don't know," Burt said.

"What've you got?" Alex said.

So he turned out his pockets, the way you do at a gate when the guard wants it all in the tray. "Leatherman," he said, and set it on the counter. "Gaffer tape." The roll, what was left of it. "Snacks." The sack. He put a knuckle up against the hard hat and left it where it was. "Hat. Postcard." The rink, with the boards and the empty seats and the scoreboard at nothing-nothing. "Token." One brass token, leaf side up.

"The remote," he said, and got it out of his back pocket.

The mannequin went wild. It swung on its base from one rack to the next to the next, pointing at the screens, all of them, every set on the wall, and then back at the remote, and then at the screens again, and the hat came down over the place where its eyes weren't.

"Okay," Burt said. "Okay, settle down."

He pointed it at a set and pressed the top button, and the button went from green to red and nothing else did anything. He went down the row and back up it, and the mannequin was rocking so hard by then that its base was walking itself across the floor a quarter-inch at a time.

"Burt," Alex said. "Show it to him."

He held the remote out, flat on his palm, near the mannequin's hand.

It leaned. Slow, a little at a time, a tilt and then another tilt, until one plastic finger was resting on a button about halfway down. It held there.

Burt pressed that one.

The finger moved. Not much — it couldn't do much — but it nudged its way over to a second button and rested on that, and waited.

"Both?" Burt said.

It bobbed.

He pointed it at the nearest set, an old Sony at chest height with Jordan standing in the middle of it, and pressed the two together.

The numbers in the corner stopped.

Burt counted, because he counted. One. The flicker had gone out of the glass; the picture just sat there, the way a photograph sits. Two. Three. Jordan's arm, up, which looked no different, which was the hard part. Four. Five.

The numbers jumped and caught up and ran on, and the flicker came back into the set like a man breathing out.

"Five seconds," Burt said.

They'd had that thing since Level One. It had pushed books out of three people's hands in PageTurn, and it would do that again. Sophia had tried it on every screen in her shop. Nobody had pressed two.

He looked out the window at the plaza and turned it over the way you turn a load before you pick it up.

"She never looked down," he said.

"No," Stacy said.

"I crawled all the way across. She never looked down once. She turned her head with me. Like she could see me fine, just not from where she was standing." He worked it slower. "And Hank waved at that big one in the ceiling, and it waved back. That one's live. That's us on it." He looked at the remote. "What if she's watching it. The live one, up top. What if that's how she knows where we are."

"She is," Djan said.

Everybody turned round.

He was still by the set near the door. He hadn't moved. His hands were at his sides.

"You knew?" Stacy said.

"I saw her looking."

Nobody said anything for a bit. Out in the plaza the Jingle came round, the little climb and the little fall, and the stop at the end of it that had started running long a while back.

Burt thought about the floor. He had been down on it with his chin on the glass, humming, and the answer had been standing behind him the whole time. He was not angry about it. He looked for that, and it wasn't there. What was there was a thing he had seen once on a job, a young fella who'd spotted a crack in a beam and didn't say, because he wasn't sure, and carried it around the site with him for a whole day like it weighed what the beam did. Burt had found him on his lunch, not eating.

A man carries a thing like that alone, it costs him. It had cost them a crawl.

"All right," Burt said. "Anything else up there I ought to know about?"

Djan looked at him. "No."

"All right."

Alex didn't say anything at all. She stood by the counter with the taped hand at her side and looked at Djan, and kept looking, and said nothing, and Burt would have taken a hollering over that any day.

---

The mannequin was beside itself.

When they turned toward the door it came after them, the base walking itself across the tile in little jerks, the whole body bobbing yes at the back of their heads, at the plaza, at the far doors. It got to the end of the counter.

It overbalanced.

Burt saw it go and was too far off. It went over forward, slow, stiff as a plank, and hit the floor all at once, and came apart — an arm one way, the trunk cracking down the seam, the hat going off on its brim in a half circle.

The head came off clean and fetched up against Alex's boot.

It was still nodding. On its side, on the tile, yes and yes, happy as anything.

"Well," Hank said, to the shop in general, "that was entirely unnecessary."

Alex crouched down and picked the head up in the taped hand. She held it out at arm's length, the way you hold a baby somebody has handed you at a party, and looked at it, and it looked back at her and nodded.

"Alas," Stacy said.

"Yorick," said Hank.

And that was its name, then.

Burt set the hat on the counter, upside down, the way you leave a man's hat, and put his things back where they lived. The remote went in his front pocket this time.

They stood in the doorway of Télévision Libertés with the bell wired open over their heads. Behind them, on every set in the room, Jordan stood with the arm up, live. Out in front, at the far end of the plaza, a woman in a green blazer stood with her head tilted up and a little out, watching the ceiling.

They had found the kid. They had a key. The key was five seconds long.

Burt had moved a lot of things in five seconds in his life. He had never once moved five people.

Alex held the head out in front of her in the silver hand, and it went on nodding at the doors. Yes. Yes.
