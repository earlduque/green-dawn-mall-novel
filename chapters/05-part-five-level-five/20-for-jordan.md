## 20 · For Jordan

> *BETACAM ½" VIDEOCASSETTE · 30 MIN. Store upright, away from heat, damp, magnetic fields, and strong feeling. Do not attempt to open the cassette shell.*

Hank had spent his whole life knowing when an engine wasn't running right, and this room was an engine that wasn't running right. He could hear it under the music.

The music was everywhere. It came out of every screen at once, out of the floor and the ceiling and the square columns and the frame of the door they had locked, and for the first time since the lift it had words. COME ON DOWN, TO GREEN DAWN, WHERE THE SAVINGS NEVER END. Somebody had recorded a singer for it, bright and close to the microphone, a voice for selling a thing to a kitchen. It shook the floor. Hank felt it come up through his boots the way you feel a truck go by on the road outside a garage.

And under it, the hitch.

He had been hearing it since the last escalator. Somewhere on the way down that thing had started backfiring in his head — a crack and a cough six feet behind him that was never there when he turned — and he had told Alex it was his and left it at that. It was here now, in the room, at the end of every bar. A small stumble where the tune came round to its stop and held there a half-beat long. It had been holding that half-beat since about the time of the ice, and he had heard it then and called it nothing. A man who tunes cars knows what nothing sounds like. This was a cylinder dropping out and catching again.

Every screen in the room was showing the six of them. Above, below, from behind, from a foot off Burt's ear, close enough to count the stitching on Stacy's bag. Thousands of pictures, and every one of them firing the same way, which was at the bag on Stacy's shoulder.

The bag was fighting her. It bucked against her hip like something in a sack, and she had both arms round it and her mouth open, and Hank could see she was humming and could not hear one note of it. Burt had a hand on Jordan's shoulder. The rope from his belt to Jordan's waist lay slack across the floor, across a picture of itself. Alex had the mannequin head under one arm and the taped hand out flat in the air, the way you try to quiet a room.

Behind them, the door was silent. Hank had stopped hearing the fist on it some time ago and only now noticed that he had.

---

The Betacam was heavier than it should have been.

He knew what that camera weighed. He had carried it up five flights in the annex and down every Level since, and it weighed what it weighed, and it did not weigh this. He shifted it on the pad and the strap found the bruise it had been making all day.

The tape. He thumbed the eject, and the cassette door came up and the cassette rose halfway out of the well on its springs, the way it does, offering itself. It was the one off the counter at Télévision Libertés — the one the plastic man had leaned toward until somebody understood, the one Hank had loaded and played and found blank. He had put it in because it was the tape there was.

There was writing on the label.

Black marker, block capitals, pressed into the middle of the white strip where you write the slate.

FOR JORDAN.

He had not written it. Nobody he could think of had been near the camera to write it. It had been blank when he loaded it, and he would have seen, because he read every label before he loaded a tape. That was the job. You read the label.

He pushed the cassette down and the door shut over it with a click.

On the side of the body the little light was blinking. Standby. It had been running when they came through the door, and at some point since then it had stopped, and he could not remember stopping it. It blinked at him about as fast as a resting heart.

It wanted to record. He could tell the way you can tell a dog wants to go out.

---

The trouble was the light.

That was where his head went first, standing in the noise with the floor shaking and a handbag trying to get away from a woman beside him: you could not white-balance this room. Every screen in it was a different temperature. The rink was blue and the painters were yellow and the break room with the donated coffee machine was the green that strip lights go, and there was no setting on any camera ever built that would make all of them read white at once. He thought about it for longer than a man should. Then he let it go, because there was no fixing it, and because it was not the problem.

He knew what to point it at. He had known since the plaza.

Jordan stood where Burt had put him, the plastic arm hanging at his side and the new pink hand held against his chest as though somebody might take it back. He was looking at the bag. Hank got the Betacam up on his shoulder and his eye into the cup and found him in the viewfinder, small and grey in the middle of the frame — the story, which was what he had been the whole time.

He pressed record.

Every screen in the room turned toward the lens.

He felt it before he saw it. It was like saying something too loud at a party, the way every head comes round together. On the walls, on the columns, on the ceiling, the pictures stopped pointing at the bag. Every one of them was now a picture of a man with a camera on his shoulder, from wherever it was taken from, and every one of those men was looking straight down the barrel at him.

Then the nearest one went black.

It didn't switch off. A set switching off gives you a white dot and a crackle. This went dark the way a glass goes empty, and Hank felt where it went. Something came out of it into the lens and down into the body of the camera and settled, and the Betacam sat heavier on his shoulder by an amount he could have put on a scale.

The next one went. Then the next.

It was like holding a bucket under a tap.

Jordan looked straight into the lens. His eyes were his own. In the viewfinder he was very small and the black was coming up behind him a screen at a time.

"Oh," Jordan said. "That's what it was for."

---

He held it as long as he could. Then his shoulder said no, the way a jack says no when you ask it for one more inch, and he brought the camera down off the pad in both arms and set it on the floor.

He set it level, lens on Jordan, still rolling. He got down on one knee beside it and kept a hand on top of the body so it would not walk. The floor under it was a screen, and the screen went black under his knee.

Then the room went.

It went out from the camera, like a ring going out across a pond. The floor first, in a widening circle. Then the walls, a panel at a time, and the columns from the bottom up, and the ceiling last, everything going from pictures to dark glass. The singing didn't stop. It got further off, as if it were being driven away down a long road.

He wasn't watching any of it. He was kneeling with his hand on a camera, and he could feel what was going into it.

First, joy. Somebody young, who had found the story of his life and could not keep still.

Then terror, all at once, and a hand going cold and not opening.

Then a long quiet.

Then three days of loneliness the size of a building.

He knelt in that one a long time. Some of it, he thought, wasn't Jordan's. He could not have said what made him think so. It was too big for one person, and it didn't know what it wanted, and it came through Jordan the way weather comes through a window. He thought it might be the mall, wanting something it had no word for. He thought that once, and did not trust it, and let it go into the tape with the rest.

Last, a thing from a minute ago. A mouthful of food somebody had made at home on a Sunday. Seven photographs coming up out of the grey. Heat laid across the back of a hand.

*Oh. They came.*

Hank felt that one too, and his hand on the camera went tight.

---

One screen was left.

It was on the far wall, a long way off — the beach, the empty one, sand and flat water and nobody on it. Everything else in the room was black glass under the light that came from nowhere, and the beach was still lit.

It didn't go black. It went pale. The water and the sand lost their colour together, evenly, as if a sun were coming up on the far side of them and getting into everything, until the panel was a square of white on a dead wall.

The Jingle stopped in the middle of a note.

Not at the end of a bar. Not at its stop, where it always stopped and always started again. In the middle of a note, like somebody had put a hand flat on the strings.

Hank had stood beside a lot of engines in the second after the key came out, and he knew that silence, and this was not it. This one kept going. It filled the room to the walls and pressed on his eardrums, and inside it he could hear Burt breathing, and the rope ticking against the floor, and nothing else at all. It was the loudest thing he had ever heard.

He looked down at the camera.

The tally light had gone steady. The counter had stopped turning. He got both hands under the body and lifted, and it came up off the floor like a toolbox somebody had packed with the wrong tools — ten pounds over what it had been, maybe more. The tape was full. He didn't need to check. He knew it the way you know a tank is full from the sound when you knock on it.

Proof. That was what he had carried the thing down here for. Proof that he'd been here, for when they got out and somebody said *prove it*.

He got it back up onto his shoulder, onto the same bruise, and he knew, getting his feet under him, that whatever was on that tape, it wasn't what anything had looked like. He knew it the way he knew an engine. He left it there.

---

Nobody decided to walk toward the white screen. They walked toward it anyway, because it was the only thing in the room to walk toward.

It took a while. The floor was dark glass now and showed nothing under their feet, and their footsteps came back off the far wall with a delay on them. Burt had Jordan by the good arm. Djan was a few steps ahead, the way Djan always was. Stacy walked with the bag clamped against her ribs and her heels going on the glass like somebody typing. Alex came last, with the head.

Jordan stopped at the foot of the white screen and turned round and looked at the camera on Hank's shoulder. Then at him. He was smiling with the whole of his face, both sides of it, which Hank had not seen it do since the office.

"Don't develop it without me," Jordan said.

"Yeah," Hank said.

---

For a second nobody moved, in all that silence.

"Stacy," somebody said. Alex, he thought.

Stacy took one arm off the bag. It hung from her shoulder and did nothing. She wet her lips and hummed.

It was the tune from Canvas & Craft, the one she couldn't put a name to, and in that quiet it went the whole width of the room and came back. She did not put the fifth bar in the wrong place this time.

On the wall behind the white screen, a line started.

It came up out of the dead glass the way a pencil line comes out on paper, and went up and across and down. It was rough. It wobbled at the corners and doubled over itself on the left, where the hand drawing it had changed its mind. It was a door the way you draw a door from memory for somebody who has never seen one: an oblong, and a dot where the handle goes.

Then the paint went deeper. Grain came into it. The dot thickened and threw a shadow and was a lever, brass, and Hank heard the latch go back.

The door swung in.

Cold air came through it first. Concrete. A whiff of somebody's exhaust. Past the frame, a strip of sky — blue, the plain afternoon kind — and a bar of daylight lying in across the dark glass at one angle, with an end on it.

The parking lot.

Nobody said how long it would hold. Every one of them had stood in front of a door like this in an art supply store and heard Alex count it out. Hank looked round and her lips were moving, and her thumb was on the inside of her own wrist.

Djan went first, without a word, the way he went everywhere. He was simply on the other side.

Then Burt, half-carrying Jordan, one arm round his back and his good hand on Burt's shoulder, and the rope between his belt and Jordan's waist went taut across the threshold as Jordan's feet came over last, and then went slack again outside.

Stacy right behind them, in the heels, the bag held shut against her ribs with both hands.

Then Hank.

He had to turn sideways to get the camera through the frame. The air on the far side smelled of gasoline and rain, wet tarmac and a dumpster somewhere, and he took a breath of it and it was the best thing he had ever smelled. The ground under his boots was asphalt. It didn't give.

He turned round to look back through the door.

It stood open in a brick wall, and through it the Screening Room was a long dark box of glass with a square of white at the near end. Alex was in it. Last, a step inside the frame, the head under her arm and the taped hand at her side, her mouth still going with the count.

Behind her, across three hundred feet of dead screens, came the small sound of the lock turning over by itself.
