## 17 · Five Seconds

> *STILL/PAUSE: Press both buttons together to hold the picture. Still will not operate on a picture already held. Please allow the unit to rest between uses.*

Hank had the red light on, and this time he had put it there on purpose.

The tally lamp sat on the nose of the Betacam above the lens, a red bead the size of a lentil, and if he tipped the camera down he could see the glow of it on the yellow floor tape. He did not tip the camera down. He had the eyepiece against his cheekbone and the blank cassette from Télévision Libertés turning in the deck, and every second of the plaza was going onto it whether anything happened in that second or not. The counter ran up in white figures in the corner of the frame. He had not shot like that since the Promenade. If he was honest he had not shot like that since the lift, when it had still been a story about a missing intern and a building with a floor too many.

Somebody, when they got out, was going to say *prove it.* He had decided on the walk back across the plaza that when they did, he was going to hand them a tape. That was the job now. Get through those doors, and have it.

---

The plan was in the room with them. Nobody had said it.

Burt had said *what if she's watching it* in the shop, slowly, the way he worked out a weight limit, and Djan had said *she is,* and after that everybody knew what they were doing the way you know a song you have heard too many times — all of it at once and none of it in order. They had come out of Télévision Libertés and walked to the tape without discussing it. Now they stood along it, five across, and Hank was framing the doors.

The tape ran across the floor twenty feet out, laid straight over the screens, so that a corridor showing on the floor beneath it had a yellow stripe through it like a road. Past the tape, a few steps short of the doors, the woman in the green blazer stood with her hands folded at her waist. She faced them. She had faced them since the escalator. Her chin was up and a little out, and her eyes were on a place above their heads, where a big screen sat flush in the centre of the ceiling, angled down toward her, so that whoever stood at those doors could watch the whole plaza without once turning round.

Hank put the ceiling in the top of the frame and the woman in the bottom of it, with the doors behind her — grey paint, two push bars, three hands of writing — and he had the whole problem in one shot. It was a good feeling. He did not trust it.

On the ceiling five people stood at a yellow line. One had a camera on his shoulder. Hank could make out the red bead on it, the size of nothing.

Under everything the hum went on in his breastbone, where the piano used to be.

Alex had Yorick under her left arm. The head had been nodding since the shop and it was still nodding, small and steady, a man agreeing with everything in a meeting.

"Are we going on three," Stacy said, "or after three?"

Nobody answered her. Hank thought about it later and decided it had been the most reasonable question anybody asked on Level Five.

Burt raised the remote.

He held it the way he held everything, as though it might have to bear weight, the grey ribbed body of it in his fist and the top button gone a colour Hank had no name for. His thumb found one of the two buttons the mannequin had found for him. His forefinger came round the side and found the other. He did not count. He pointed it at the ceiling and pressed them both together, and the plastic gave under his thumb with a click you could hear over the hum.

On the ceiling, five people went perfectly still.

They had been still already. That was the joke of it, and Hank did not get the joke until afterward. Stacy had her hand halfway up to her hair. Down on the floor she finished the gesture and put the hand back down. Up on the ceiling she didn't. Up there her hand stayed where it was, an inch off her ear, and would stay there.

The woman's head did not move. She was watching five statues.

In the held picture Hank found her too, at the bottom edge — the shoulders of the blazer, the green sun on its lapel, and her face tipped back toward the lens, the only face in the picture looking up at whatever had taken it.

Then Burt and Alex went.

---

Alex went first by half a step, Yorick tucked in hard against her ribs like a ball she meant to score with, the head bouncing on every stride and still nodding between bounces. Burt went after her with his chin down under the yellow brim and the remote held out in front of him, the way a man carries a set of keys into a dark house. Neither of them looked back. Neither of them had said anything.

Hank had it.

He had it all in the viewfinder: the frozen ceiling across the top, two people running through the middle, and the woman at the bottom with her eyes on a picture of nothing moving. Three layers, all true, all at once. Every edit he had ever sat up late over had been hunting for that shot.

He did not go.

Djan was beside him on the left, not moving. Stacy was beside him on the right, not moving. Nobody had said *go.* Hank had been counting since Burt's thumb went down, without deciding to, the way you count off a take — and he was at two when he understood that the count was for him.

He ran.

He ran with the Betacam on his shoulder because there was no time to do anything else with it, the eyepiece knocking his cheekbone, the frame going up and down like a boat. Off to his left he heard Djan's shoes come off the line. Off to his right Stacy's heels started up on the screen-floor, a sound like somebody typing fast.

Burt went past the woman's right shoulder with a foot to spare. She did not turn. He got as far as the doors and swung round with his back to them, so that the woman was between him and the rest of them, and Hank saw him see it — three people still out in the open, fifteen feet of floor, and nothing left on the count. Burt lifted the remote over her head at the ceiling and pressed both buttons again.

Nothing happened.

The buttons went in. Hank could see them go in. They went in the way a lift button goes in when it is already lit, all the way down and back up, telling you nothing. Burt looked at the remote like a tool that had stopped doing the one thing it did. Nobody had ever pointed it at anything twice.

Alex hit the left-hand door with her shoulder and the taped hand flat on the bar.

It was not locked. The bar went down under her palm with a clack and the door swung in, away from her, and she went with it as far as the threshold and stopped there and looked in.

Hank had her at the left edge of the frame, at a run, out of focus.

The light from inside came out over her face in pieces. It changed colour faster than he could have named the colours, a lot of small pictures going over her at once. Her face didn't do anything. That was what he got. Alex's face did things all the time — it took notes, it disbelieved you, it waited politely for you to finish being wrong — and now nobody was running it. Her mouth came open a little and stayed open. She did not blink. Her eyes went wet, both at once, and she still did not blink, and her free hand came off the bar and went flat against the door beside her, the way it had gone flat against the steel wall of the lift on the first day, without her deciding it.

Under her arm, Yorick nodded.

Hank had put a lens on a great many people finding something out. He had never got one like that, and he had got it out of focus, from the side, bouncing.

On the ceiling, the picture moved.

---

"What the *hell?*"

Her chin never came down. Hank saw that and would keep seeing it. Whatever she had on the ceiling now — a big man in a hard hat at her back, a woman in her door, three people coming at her across the floor — she got all of it from up there, and she answered all of it with her eyes still on the screen. Her right hand went under the blazer and came out with the baton and flicked it, and it went out to its full length with the sound of a car aerial in a cold snap.

She swung at the nearest thing, and the nearest thing was Burt.

He was behind her. She went round for him backhanded, whole-bodied, from the heels, and Burt ducked the way a big man ducks, by folding at the knees all at once, and the baton went through the space where the hard hat had been with a hand's width to spare. Hank heard it from ten feet. It made a sound in the air like a sheet being snapped off a line.

She had put everything into it. Everything went with it. The swing carried her round past Burt and kept carrying her, half a turn and then more, her heels skidding on the screen-floor, until she came to a stop square to the grey doors with her back to the plaza, her back to the ceiling, her back to Hank.

It was the swing of somebody who had been waiting a long time to be allowed one. That was Hank's guess, watching her try to haul it back. It might have been wrong.

Her head went up anyway. It went up and out at the wall above the doors, where a screen showed an escalator with nobody on it, and stayed there, hunting.

Hank was three feet behind her. He had not seen himself get there.

Djan broke left. He went wide around her at a run and slapped the wall-screens as he passed them, flat-handed, crack, crack, crack, a sound Hank would not have guessed Djan had in him. Stacy broke right, heels going, and she had her voice up at the size she used for the top of a piece — "Over *here,* sweetheart, over here, *hi*" — and the woman's head went left after the slapping and then right after Stacy and then left again, the baton coming up and not knowing where to go.

The doorway was behind her. Alex was in it, and Burt beside it with his back to the wall and the remote still up like it might yet change its mind.

Hank knew what he wanted. He wanted her on the floor and out of that doorway. Not hurt. Not more than that. And if he didn't, it was the four of them on this side of the doors with her and that baton, and it would be him who had stood at the tape getting the shot.

He took the camera off his shoulder.

The last time he had put it down he had put it down on its side, on the carpet of the Promenade, the way you never set a Betacam down, and had not thought about it afterward. He thought about it now. He set it upright on the floor on its own base, the lens toward the doors, the red light on, the tape still turning.

Then he dropped his shoulder and went into her from the side.

---

She was heavier than he was ready for, solid all the way down in the way of a person who has done one thing for a long time, and she was not braced, because she was turned the wrong way listening for Stacy. His shoulder went in under her ribs. The blazer was some synthetic that squeaked against his jacket. They went over together for half a step and then she went on without him, off her feet, sliding on the screen-floor on her side with a long rubber squeal, a fountain going by underneath her, then a corridor, then the ice.

She hit the wall beside the doors back-first. Her left hand was out to catch herself and it caught the wall-screen flat, at an angle hands are not meant to meet anything, and Hank heard it.

He had expected more of her. He had braced, the whole run in, for something built out of three hundred years, and he had hit a woman in a blazer, and she had gone down like one.

She came up onto her knees. She had the hand against her chest, folded into the green lapel under the pin, and her mouth was pulled tight with it, and the baton was lying on the floor screen a yard off where she had let it go. Her head went up. She twisted on her knees to find the ceiling, and found it, and stayed like that, looking up and out over Hank's head at the plaza on the screen.

Hank knew what was on it without looking. Djan going in. Stacy going in, heels last. Burt going in sideways with the remote. The four of them, small, from above, going through her doors.

He picked the camera up on the way past. He did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not come out as a line, and he did not have a line for her.

He felt sorry for her. It came up in him on the threshold, plain and complete, and there was nowhere on him to put it. He carried it in.

He went through backward, with the camera back on his shoulder and still on her — a woman on her knees beside a door, holding her hand, watching a ceiling — and the door came round past his elbow and shut on her an inch from the lens, and the frame went flat grey.

Alex had slammed it. She was right there at his side in the light coming off everything, with Yorick under one arm and her taped hand already on the door, feeling down it. There was a thumb-turn under the handle on this side, brass, worn pale in the middle. She found it and threw it, and the bolt went across with a heavy sound, the most ordinary sound Hank had heard in a long while.

The door jumped in its frame. Then again.

"No!" Through the metal it came out small and high, every end still going up. "No, no — I'm going to get in *so* much trouble!"

Alex put her mouth near the door. She did not raise her voice.

"Who's an employee now, bitch."

Hank had never once heard her swear. Nobody behind him said anything at all.

The banging stopped. There was a pause, long enough that Hank thought that was all.

"...what?"

---

He was still facing the door.

The lens was an inch off the grey paint and the frame was grey, and the red light was on, and in the viewfinder the counter went on running up in the corner over a picture of nothing. He had the whole of the last minute on tape. He had her, and the ceiling, and Alex's face at the edge of the frame, out of focus. He had it.

On the paint in front of him the colours were moving. They came over his shoulders from behind and went across the door in patches, a lot of small lights at once, changing faster than he could have named them, the way they had gone over Alex's face. He could feel them on the back of his neck. They came from something very large, and close, and they were not still for even a second.

Nobody behind him had moved.

Hank turned around.
