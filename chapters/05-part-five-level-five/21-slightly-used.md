## 21 · Slightly Used

> *GREEN DAWN MALL · THANK YOU FOR SHOPPING · ALL SALES FINAL · NO REFUNDS OR EXCHANGES ON MERCHANDISE THAT HAS LEFT THE PREMISES · PLEASE RETAIN YOUR RECEIPT*

Alex was counting the door. She had started when the handle went solid, the way she started on any door with four other people and a price on it, and she stood at the back of the dark room with a mannequin's head under one arm and watched the rest of them go through.

Djan first, at a run, not looking back, which was the one time on five Levels she had wanted him a corridor ahead and not coming back. Then Burt, with Jordan against his side like a sack of something he had agreed to carry to the car, and the rope between them going straight over the threshold. Stacy behind them with the bag clamped under her elbow. Hank last of the four, bent sideways under the Betacam, both arms round it.

Four, and Jordan. Five through. She put them on the list in that order, and the list was correct, and there was one name left on it.

Through the doorway was asphalt, and white lines, and a grey sky with a strip of blue along the bottom of it. Cold came in off it in a sheet. It smelled of gasoline and wet pavement, and her whole body leaned toward it a quarter of an inch, and she let it lean and did not move her feet.

She was last. She had been last at the top of every escalator and the foot of every one, and she had told herself it was because last is where you can see everybody, and that had been true, and it had not been the whole of it. Somebody had to see the others out. Somebody had to be standing in the room when it was empty, to say so. It had taken her five Levels to notice that she had given the job to herself, and nobody had argued, which meant they had all known.

Behind her the room was dead. Three hundred feet of screens and not one of them lit — floor, ceiling, the square columns going up out of sight, all of it the flat grey-black of a television in a shop after closing. The only light came in through the door and lay across the floor toward her in one long piece. There was no piano. She stood in the first silence since the lift and found she was holding her breath so as not to spend any of it.

The door had started thinning at the corners. Not much. The way a painted line goes when somebody has been over it with a wet rag.

Behind her, across the whole dark width of the room, the lock turned over by itself.

---

She heard it the way you hear a key in your own front door when you live alone. Metal on metal, the bar going down, the hinge.

The guard came in limping.

She came the whole three hundred feet. Alex watched her do it, because there was nothing to stop her and nowhere for Alex to go except the door, and the door was for after. The woman's left hand was held flat against her chest, fingers curled in, the way you hold a hand that has been shut in a car door. The baton was out in the other, at its full length. The green blazer had torn at the shoulder seam and the lining showed through, a paler green, and the pin on the lapel was still on straight.

Her head was tilted up. A little up and a little out, the way it had been at the tape, the way it had been, Alex supposed, for a very long time. She came on with her face lifted to the ceiling, and the ceiling was dark, and about forty feet out she stopped walking.

She stood there with her chin up. Alex watched her look for the picture, and not find it, and look again in the same place.

Then she lowered her head and looked at Alex.

Straight at her. Level, the way people look at each other across a counter. It took a long time and it did not come easily; Alex could see the work of it in her neck.

And something came in from the left.

Alex knew it before she moved her eyes. It had stood at the edge of her vision since the top of the last escalator, a person's height, a person's patience, always on whichever side she wasn't looking. Now it came round out of the corner of her eye at a walk, unhurried, into the light from the door, and stepped into place beside the guard. For a second there were two of them, one sharp and one not. Then the second settled into the first like a coat going onto a hook, and there was one woman in a torn blazer, looking at her.

It had been her. The whole way down, it had been her, watching.

Alex filed that. There was nowhere to put it yet, and she filed it anyway.

"Okay," the guard said.

Her voice was shaking. She got it under control the way a person does on the second day of a new job, by holding on to the script. "Okay. I can't stop the customers. The customers have loyalty cards." A breath. "That's — fine. That's policy."

The oval on the inside of Alex's left forearm, under the sleeve, did nothing at all. She felt it there anyway.

The guard brought the baton up and pointed it past her. Past Alex's shoulder, through the door, out into the parking lot. Alex did not have to look to know at what.

"But that one doesn't have a card," the guard said. "That one has been on the floor for three days. Under Mall policy, that's merchandise." Her voice went up at the end the way it always had, a question that already knew its answer, and then it did not come back down. "And nobody — *nobody* — walks out of Green Dawn Mall with unpaid merchandise. Not on my shift."

Out on the asphalt, Jordan's feet moved.

He had not taken a step. His sneakers slid, both together, flat to the ground, a few inches back toward the door, the way a box slides on a truck bed when the truck brakes. Jordan looked down at them. Burt said something Alex could not hear and set his boots and leaned back, and the rope came up off the ground and went hard as a bar, and Jordan's sneakers kept sliding anyway, slower, an inch, another inch.

The edges of the door were going back to paint. She could see the brushwork coming up through the frame, the strokes lying one way along the top and another way down the sides, like a wall that has had a door painted on it and is remembering that it was a wall.

Half her count was gone. She had the number exactly and did not need it, because she could see it in the frame.

---

The guard was coming toward her. Not fast — one step, then another, the baton out in front, and she had brought the bad hand up under the good one to steady it, the way you hold something too heavy for one wrist. She was pointing it at Jordan. She was coming to stand in the door.

Alex did not decide. She had not decided in PageTurn either, with Djan's thumb riding the pages in time to the piano. Her right hand came up in its tape, flat, edge-on, with no wind-up at all, and came down across the baton where the woman's two hands were wrapped round it.

It landed on the bad one.

The guard made a sound — not a yell, something smaller, the noise of a person who has touched the wrong side of a pan — and her fingers opened, and the baton went out of both her hands and hit the dead floor and went skittering off into the dark, end over end, a long way. Alex heard it stop against a column.

The guard did not go after it.

She stood there. The left hand had gone back to her chest and she was holding it with the right, and she was shaking from the shoulders down, and she was still looking at Alex.

Alex looked back and saw it, plainly, the way she had seen it on the man at the Servants' Desk who had kept smiling while he went somewhere else. The guard had never been going to swing. Not at the tape, not now. Alex would have bet the paper that in all that time on the floor the only thing the woman had ever hit was the air beside Burt. She was not angry. She was frightened — not of Alex. Of the next thing. Of whatever happened to the one person on the floor who let the merchandise walk.

Alex had been a fixer long enough to know what that face wanted. It did not want to win. It wanted a way to have done its job.

She bent and put Yorick down on the floor by the guard's feet to free her hand, and the head rocked once toward the guard and settled.

Her left hand went into her coat pocket. The quarters were long gone, spent on four postcards and a magnet in a shop with a boy behind the register. What was left down there was brass: the tokens out of the Atrium fountain, carried down every escalator for no reason she had ever been able to give, gone warm from riding against her side. A leaf on one face. GREEN DAWN and *1 TOKEN* on the other. Nobody had wanted one. The boy had turned one down.

She brought out a fistful and did not count it. It was the first thing in five Levels she had not counted.

She took the guard's good hand by the wrist, turned it palm up, put the tokens in it, and closed the fingers over them with her own.

"There," Alex said. "That one's paid for. You did your job."

---

The guard looked down at her hand.

Then something went through her from the top down, and Alex watched it go — the chin first, coming level; then the shoulders; then the spine. She straightened the blazer with one tug at the hem, torn shoulder and all. She put the tokens in her outside pocket without looking at them. From an inside pocket she took a receipt pad, green, with a carbon sheet under the top leaf, and a pen on a short chain, and she wrote.

She wrote very fast. She wrote like somebody who had been trained to do it ten thousand times and had never once been asked.

She tore the top sheet off along the perforation in one clean pull and held it out.

ONE (1) INTERN. SLIGHTLY USED.

Alex read it twice. A quick hand, a training-course hand. A line for the date with nothing on it. A line for a signature with nothing on it. No total. Alex had been handed a great many receipts in her working life, for cabs and coffees and a hotel in a town nobody should have to visit, and she had never once been handed one without a total, and of everything that had happened in the last minute, that was the part that bothered her.

Somewhere very deep in the mall, under her feet, under all the Levels she had come down and whatever was under those, a cash register rang.

It was the bright one. The drawer-open bell, *ka-ching*, a happy sound, a Saturday sound. It came up through the floor and down through the ceiling and in from every wall at once, and Alex understood, with no way of knowing it, that it had rung on every floor at the same moment — the Atrium, the bookshop, the rink, the Gallery, the plaza outside the door — and that everybody down here had heard it.

Out on the asphalt, Jordan's sneakers stopped.

Burt took two steps backward that he had not meant to take and caught himself, with Jordan against his chest and the rope gone slack between them, and Jordan put his real hand flat on Burt's shoulder and laughed, once, like a cough.

Paid for. Alex looked at the slip in her hand. Whether she now owned an intern was a question for later.

---

The guard composed herself. She folded her hands at her waist, the bad one inside the good one, lifted her chin a quarter of an inch, and said it in the bright voice, every syllable where it belonged.

"Thank you for your business. Enjoy the screening."

She had said it, Alex thought, ten million times. You could hear the groove it had worn. And it came out of her the same as all the other times and was not the same at all, the way a phrase you have said your whole life will mean something different the one day you need it to.

Then, quieter, for Alex, with nobody else in the room to hear it:

"…Who's an employee now."

Her own line. She had said it through a locked door, with a word on the end she had never said out loud at work. It came back to her without the word, and it came back as a kindness.

And the guard smiled.

It came up slowly and a little crooked, as though the muscles for it had been put away somewhere and had to be found, and then it stayed. Alex had the strong sense that it was the first time in a very long while, and that the woman was as surprised by it as she was.

Alex, who had filed Jordan's assignment herself on a Tuesday in about a minute and a half, took the receipt and folded it once along its length and closed her taped hand on it. The sum came out. She had been running it since a poster rack on Level One — what does it cost, who pays, is the thing on the far side worth the price — and it had never once come out, because the term she needed always turned out to be a person. Here it was, in carbon, in somebody else's handwriting. One. Slightly used. Paid.

The door was nearly gone. The handle had gone flat, a painted handle with a painted shine on it.

She stepped through.

---

Asphalt under her boots. Cold air, and gasoline, and rain.

Behind her the door shut with a soft click, like a book being closed by somebody who had finished it.

She turned round.

Brick. Red brick, the back wall of a building, the mortar gone dark in the joints, a downpipe, a stain down the face where something had leaked for years. At about the height of a door handle there was a smear of paint on it, a little green and a little white, about the size of a thumb.

Alex stood and looked at the wall, with the receipt in her taped hand.
