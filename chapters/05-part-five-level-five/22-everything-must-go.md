## 22 · Everything Must Go

> *SHOE SALE — THIS WEEKEND ONLY. Comfortable shoes for the whole family. Every style, every size. Everything must go.*

Burt Calloway had the kid's weight against his left shoulder and sixty feet of Djan's rope knotted to his belt, and he was counting. Three on the asphalt in front of him, one on his shoulder, and Alex turning round from the wall with a slip of paper in her taped hand.

Six.

He had come in with five. He counted again, because he would have counted again whatever it came to, and it came out six the second time, and he stood there and let it be six.

Behind Alex was a brick wall. Common brick, a soldier course along the top, a downpipe with one bracket gone and the pipe leaning off it. At about the height of a door handle somebody had smeared a little paint on the brick, a little green and a little white, the size of a thumb. That was all there was of the door.

Alex looked at it for a while. Then she looked at the paper.

"Alex," Burt said.

"Yeah."

"You good."

"I'm good." She held the slip up a little, the way you hold a ticket at a deli counter when they're about to call your number, not to show it to anybody. Just so it was up.

---

It was afternoon. Overcast, the grey kind that sits on a place all day and never does anything about it, and nobody would have pulled the car over to take a picture of it. There was a sun up there. It was behind the cloud a little left of the annex roof, a paler patch in the grey, and Burt could have put his finger on it.

They were standing on a strip of walkway down the side of the mall, where the annex joined on, between a dumpster enclosure with its gate off one hinge and a fire door with no handle on the outside. Out past the corner the lot ran off in white lines. Four cars. One of them theirs, where they'd left it, nose to the curb.

Through the glass of the annex lobby he could see the sawhorse. Same sawhorse, same length of pipe balanced across it, the stack of ceiling tiles going soft at the corners. The notice was still taped to the wall. From here he couldn't read the date on it, but he knew what it said and he knew it had already happened. Nobody had come back for that job. Burt looked at it the way he'd look at any job left standing: two men, a week, he'd have it closed out. The pipe wasn't tied off to anything. He noted that too.

"Burt," the kid said, into his jacket. "I can stand."

"Sure."

"I can, though."

He let some weight off, the way you let a load down onto a pallet, a little and then a little more, his arm still round him, until Jordan's sneakers had it. Canvas sneakers, grey at the toes, laces done in a double knot. They held. They were just sneakers.

Djan came and undid the knot at Burt's belt. It took him one pull, because it was Djan's knot. He coiled the rope elbow to hand while the rest of them stood there, sixty feet of it going round and round, and hung the coil on his shoulder, and put his hands back in his pockets.

Hank had the Betacam up on his shoulder and it was riding wrong. Burt could see it from ten feet — the strap biting, the whole of Hank tipped a few degrees toward that side like a truck with a bad spring.

"Give me that," Burt said.

"No."

"It's heavy, Hank. I can see it's heavy."

"I know it's heavy."

Hank moved it over to the other shoulder and kept it. Burt had been handed everybody's everything since the stairwell on the fourth floor, and that was the first load anyone had kept off him the whole way down, and he found he didn't mind it.

---

They went round the corner to the front because that was where the car was.

The mall doors were the same double glass, with the bar across, and as they came up a woman pushed out through them with a shoebox under her arm. She had a fleece on and her keys already in her hand. She looked at six people coming round the side of the building from the dumpsters — one in heels, one in a hard hat, one with an arm that was plastic to the collarbone and hung like it — and decided that none of it was her business, which Burt thought was the correct decision, and went to her car.

When the doors opened, music came out with her. A man singing something soft over a saxophone, a song Burt half-knew from a dentist's waiting room or a hardware store. It had nothing to do with a piano.

He found he was standing there waiting for it to come back round to the start.

The doors closed on it halfway through a line. A minute later they opened again for a kid on a phone, and the song was further along, up into a chorus, and the next time they opened it was a different song, and it never did go back.

There was an A-frame sign on the walkway by the doors. SHOE SALE — THIS WEEKEND ONLY. Comfortable shoes for the whole family. Burt did not remember it. He would have remembered it, because an A-frame on a walkway by a door is a thing somebody catches a foot on, and he'd have moved it to the wall. Either somebody had put it out since, or he'd walked past it with everybody's bags on him and not seen it, and he didn't much care for either.

Stacy read it and stood up a little straighter in her heels.

"Comfortable shoes," she said, like a woman reading out a charge sheet.

Past the sign, through the glass, the concourse ran back toward the north end. The fountain was there in the middle of it, off. Change in the bottom under an inch of standing water, coppers and silver and a few that had gone green, and one of the ceiling lights making a long shine across the top of it. It looked like what it was, a fountain somebody had stopped paying to run.

Djan looked at it longer than it needed. Alex looked at it and then at the receipt.

---

All six phones went off at once.

Buzzing in coat pockets, in Hank's back pocket, a chime out of Stacy's jacket, and something in Jordan's jeans that made Jordan jump like he'd been touched on the shoulder. Everybody looked down. That was a thing Burt had not seen six people do together in a long while, and it was ordinary, and he watched it.

His own had four texts on it and a voicemail and a message telling him his storage was nearly full. The texts were people asking where he was. The time across the top of the screen said a time, and the time agreed with the sky.

Stacy stood with hers a long time. She went up through it with her thumb, slowly, a long way up, and came back down.

"They're in order," she said, to the phone.

Nobody asked what. She went to drop it in the Neverfull, stopped with her hand over the zip, and put it in her jacket instead. The bag sat on her shoulder and did nothing. She kept her other hand flat on it anyway.

"Eleven," somebody said behind him, not loud. "Eleven times."

When Burt turned round, one of them was already walking — phone up to the ear, out across the lot past the cart corral and the light pole and on past that, out where the lines gave up, and stood there with their back to everyone. Burt knew the back. It wasn't his to carry, so he didn't, and he looked at Jordan instead.

He put a hand up to the brim of the hat. Still on. Nobody had said a word about it, not once, not since the trunk, and nobody said one now. He didn't take it off. A man takes his hat off when the job's done, and nobody had told him what this job was any more. He was still in charge of something. He couldn't have told you what.

---

Hank got the Betacam down off his shoulder and onto the hood of the car, which Burt allowed, and flipped the little monitor out on its arm and said "Okay," to nobody, and pressed play.

They came in round him without being asked. Five heads over one little screen, the way a crew stands round a man reading a printout.

It was the security office first. The one on Level One, the stacked sets, the screens running a few seconds behind the room. Then a lot of floor. Then the rink — the ice, the boards, two dozen visors, the brooms going. The camera swung and caught Burt's own back, and he looked at the size of himself from behind and didn't comment. Then a wall with a door painted on it, and paint, and four people in aprons not looking up.

That was all it was. Nothing on it moved that shouldn't. It looked like what you'd get if you sent a man with a camera into a closed-down mall for an afternoon — bad light, too much floor, the picture going soft at the edges when he walked. Nobody could have said any of it wasn't there.

And under it there was something else.

Burt felt it before he knew he was feeling it. It came up off the tape the way heat comes up off a road at six in the evening after a day of sun, and it went into his chest, into the place where the hum had been sitting all the way down, where it had sat so long he'd stopped noticing it was there. On the rink footage he felt the rink. Not the cold. The other thing, the one he'd filed on the skate counter and not taken back out. It was all there on a tape that was half an hour long, and none of it was in the picture.

It didn't show what the place had looked like. It showed what it had been like to stand in it.

Hank watched with his mouth a little open.

"It's boring," he said.

He sounded happier than Burt had ever heard him sound about anything.

"Is that—" Stacy said.

"Yeah."

Hank stopped it after the door. He folded the monitor back in against the body of the camera, and put a hand on top of it, and left it there.

---

Burt stood with that in his chest and did the thing he did at the end of a shift, which was go back over what had got used.

A stack of photographs that came up warm. A curling iron. A tape. A handful of brass tokens nobody had wanted, out of a fountain that ran over. And the snack pack, with food in it he'd made at home on a Sunday, that he'd carried down every Level and offered round at the top of every set of stairs and the foot of every escalator, and that had done one job, once, and it had been the right job.

Five things. One of them his.

He went down the list again. It came out the same. He went down it a third time, the way you go back over a load sheet that balances when you were dag-on sure it wouldn't, running your finger down the column looking for the line you added wrong. There wasn't one. It balanced.

He let it.

Then he looked at the car, because the car was the next thing.

Five seat belts. He'd counted them on the way in without meaning to, because he counted belts. Six of them now. He worked it through. The kid up front, on account of the arm, with the seat back a notch. Hank behind with the camera across his knees. Stacy would want the window, and Alex would want the other window so she could look out of it and not talk. That put Djan in the middle, and that still left one, and the one was Burt, and Burt did not fit in the middle of anything.

Somebody was going to be sitting on somebody. He thought about what the law had to say on that and decided he could live with the law for the distance.

---

Jordan had walked out into the lot on his own.

Burt hadn't seen him go. He was standing between two sets of white lines in the middle of all that grey, with nobody parked within forty feet of him. The plastic arm hung down at his side, smooth, and a little see-through at the fingers where the light from the cloud got into it. The other hand was up flat over his eyes, the real one, still wrinkled at the tips, holding the glare off. There wasn't much glare. He was looking at the sky anyway.

There was nothing up there to look at. Cloud, and the pale place where the sun was.

He looked at it for a long time.

Nobody went out to him. Hank had his hand on the camera and didn't lift it. Stacy had stopped touching her bag. Out past the light pole the one on the phone had their head down and was nodding at somebody who couldn't see it.

Burt got the backpack off his shoulders, because his hands were empty and he didn't know what else to do with them. He'd come in with five chuckleheads and a snack pack nobody would touch. He set the pack on the hood of the car next to the Betacam.

A car went by out on the road. Out past the light pole the phone came down, and went into a pocket, and the one holding it started back across the lot, not hurrying. A shopping cart in the corral ticked against the one in front of it in a little wind. The doors behind them opened and let out the end of a song, and closed again.

Jordan took the hand down off his eyes and turned round and looked at the five of them by the car, and seemed to count them.

"…We should call the paper," Jordan said.

Nobody moved for a second.

"Yeah," Alex said. She had her phone out already. She didn't dial.

Jordan came back across the lines toward them, not quickly, the arm swinging a little with each step the way a thing swings that you're carrying rather than wearing. He stopped at the front of the car and looked at the backpack on the hood, and then up at Burt.

"Is it weird that I'm starving?"

Burt was already unzipping the backpack.
